# Tools That Change the Way We Live

这一篇讲工具怎么一点点进入生活：网络、机器、软件、故障，以及人在这些东西旁边慢慢变成了什么样子。

## The Machine on the Desk

My **computer** came in a brown box on a cold Tuesday in November. I was nineteen, and the **technology** inside it felt like the future itself, folded flat. It had a loud fan, a hard drive, and a **screen** that glowed like a small aquarium. My father, who spent thirty years repairing **motor** parts, came into my room to look at it. He said the noise was normal. He was right, and it was not normal.

I typed my first line of **code** that winter. Three lines, and they printed my name. Nothing happened for a long moment, and then the words came out. That small surprise never gets old. A machine reads an instruction you wrote, and does exactly what you said.

Thirty years later I keep three **devices** on the same desk. One is for work, one is for reading, and one is so old that it no longer makes any **connection** to anything. Its **memory** is full of files made by a system that no longer exists. I keep it anyway, because keeping it feels like keeping a photograph of how I used to think.

Every one of these machines has an **engine** inside, usually one small **electronic** chip no bigger than a fingernail. And behind every machine there is **software**, the part you never see, which decides what the machine will actually be willing to do.

Back then the whole **industry** agreed on one measure: how fast the machine ran. **Artificial intelligence** arrived later and changed the question. When a machine can recognize a face or finish a sentence, speed stops being the interesting number, and nobody quite knows what to measure **instead**.

My uncle worked in a factory for twenty-two years. When the machines came in, he was afraid, and he was right to be. But he was also, **against** every expectation, the person who could hear a bearing going bad from the next room. The machine measured vibration; his **ear** measured it first. Nobody wrote that skill down, so when he retired it left with him. That is the real cost of a tool, and it never appears in the reports.

Nobody in that factory **expected** to feel lucky. They just wanted the shift to end.

## The Network Is Not in the Sky

When people say a thing is **online**, they often lift their eyes, as if the internet were weather. But a network is not weather. A network is rooms.

There is a **server** in some cold building somewhere. Every photo you send it travels down a **wire**, into a pole, up a cable, across an ocean, and down again — a **signal** hopping from one machine to the next until it finally lands somewhere that is not in your house. The whole trip takes less time than it takes you to let go of the mouse.

Somewhere on that trip, your picture joins a **database**, which is really just a very large table with a strict sense of tidiness. Open a **website** and your device sends a small request into the dark, and something answers almost immediately, as if it had been waiting for you. It had not been waiting. It had not even been awake.

Companies talk about **storage** as though it were cupboard space. It is not. Everything you keep — the photographs, the messages, the embarrassing drafts from 2011 — goes into storage, and it does not get heavier when you add more.

There is also a **log**. Every server writes one, recording who asked for what and when. Engineers read these logs the way other people read a diary, looking for the one strange line that does not belong. Almost every terrible thing can be found in a log, if someone has the patience to read it. **Furthermore**, the log is the only witness that never gets tired and never asks for a favor in return.

None of this was designed in one piece. The **web** grew because thousands of separate people decided their own small corner should connect to somebody else's. **Somehow**, out of all that disorder, the **web** ended up more useful than any central plan could have made it.

This is worth sitting with for a minute. A **solid** piece of modern knowledge usually looks like a stack of small decisions nobody **properly** controlled. That is not a failure of planning; it is what planning looks like when it works at a large scale.

And it is not free. **Among** the people who gained from all this speed, there are people who lost something and never got a **comment** on it. Their side of the story was not recorded, because the system was not **designed** to record it. To **recognize** that is not the same as to fix it, but at least it gives the problem a name.

The result we all live inside is **eventually** going to be **judged**, probably by people writing a **chapter** in a book that does not exist yet.

## Software Is a Shape You Can Change

Software is not really a set of instructions. It is a shape. Someone decides what ought to be possible, and the shape of the program decides which things are easy and which are painful.

When I first tried to **install** a piece of software, it asked me for a password I did not have, so I gave up and closed the window. The next year I wrote a small one myself, badly, and it worked. That is the whole secret of this technology: the wall is usually just a door with no handle on your side.

An **update** is a small promise. We found some mistakes; we fixed some mistakes. The trouble is that an update can quietly break the arrangement you had learned to live with. The button moves. The word you liked is gone. Two days later you find the setting buried three menus deep, and you feel briefly foolish, then relieved, then old.

Some evenings the machine simply **freezes**. The screen goes still, the small spinning circle keeps turning, and nothing answers. Nothing you do now matters. You save your work, or you do not, and you wait.

That is a **crash**. It is not a disease. It is only a program asking for more memory than it has, or making one small error a thousand times too fast. It is **bad**, and it is completely ordinary. A **frequent** crash is a habit, not an accident.

A **virus** is a different animal. A virus is **code** written by someone who wants a door open, and it travels the way a rumour travels through a market town — passed along, unnoticed, until suddenly the whole street knows.

The panic afterwards is always the same shape, and I have watched it **three** times now. First nobody believes it. Then everybody believes it. Then everybody believes the opposite, just as firmly, and **strongly** denies that the machine was ever a problem at all.

There is also the quieter **trap**: the piece of software that is not malicious, merely helpful, and quietly opens a door nobody checked. No **arrest**, no headline, no punishment. Just a small **acquisition** of information that leaves without asking.

## The Paperwork Has Moved In

Not long ago the important parts of a life were made of paper. Now they are **digital**, and that change sounds small until the day you try to buy a train ticket without a phone in your pocket.

Almost everything now wants an **account**. An account is a promise in both directions: I will remember who you are, and you will tell me when you come back. It is also, quietly, a **profile** — a shape of habits that belongs to you whether you are watching or not. Some of that shape was assembled by people you will never meet.

**Security** is the part that everybody agrees to ignore. A long password, a second step, a phone number you do not use for strangers. Each of these takes about four minutes. Each of them is skipped by nearly everyone, including the man who wrote this paragraph.

Money moved the same way. A **financial** record used to be a line in a paper book that a person wrote by hand and could misread. Now it is a row that updates itself and never closes. You can watch the number rise and fall like a temperature, and you do not have to be good at arithmetic, because the machine is.

You will **realize**, later, that the old drawer of **tape** was the last of its kind in the house. My father threw out the cassettes when the player broke. He did not save the music. He did not seem to miss it. But he kept the empty box for almost a year, and I did not ask why, and now it is too late to ask.

## The People Behind the Machine

Behind every tool there are people, and they are almost never visible. A **researcher** decides what is worth trying. A developer decides what will be tried. An **analyst** decides what the numbers mean, and is often the last person to know whether anyone wanted the numbers at all.

It takes years. Before the first version exists, a team has to agree on the **criteria** — what counts as working, what counts as good enough. Those criteria are dull, and everything depends on them. A **strict** test written carelessly can kill a good idea before it grows. A loose one lets a bad idea ship for years and call itself a feature.

These people **implement**, they correct, they **revise**, and then they do it again. **Implementation** is never finished, only postponed. A good engineer wants the first version small enough to fail quickly, because a fast failure teaches more than a slow success, and because **efficiency** without honesty about what is still broken is just tidy rubble.

Behind them sits a **corporation**, which wants the thing to make money, and a **sponsor**, and a **military** customer with a very different meaning of security. All of these pull in different directions. Sometimes they agree and something useful appears. Mostly they do not agree, and the argument shows up later in your life as a menu you cannot quite understand, on a phone that costs more than a month of rent.

The money is strange here too. Some of this work is **commercial** and pays well. Some is a **pilot** with a small grant, hoping to become something bigger. Some belongs to a **scholar** who may spend a decade on a system nobody will ever see and consider the decade well spent. Whether a person is paid for this or not, the culture loudly rewards the visible version and quietly pays for the rest.

None of this is glamorous, and the pay is only part of it. The real **incentive**, **nowadays**, is a strange mixture: money, of course, but also the **approval** of people you respect, and the **joy** of seeing a thing that did not exist this morning now work.

And some people **devote** themselves to it for another reason entirely. They like the **discipline**. They like that a problem has a right answer somewhere, even when nobody has found it yet.

## The Deals Nobody Reads

Everything above rests on paper, and paper is where the truth usually hides.

A long **federal** rule may **contain** one sentence that decides whether a small company can **expand** or must go quiet. A trade agreement may give one **province** an advantage that another province will spend ten years **oppose** in court. A **proposal** written in polite language can quietly remove a choice from four million people who will never read it.

Nobody designs this on purpose, and **nevertheless** it happens, because systems reward the people who are **firmly** in them. To change a rule you need a **majority**, or at least a very loud **segment** of people who were not paying attention until the rule hurt them.

Watch how a **debate** becomes a **bid**, and a bid becomes a **transfer** of money, and the money decides what gets built. A single **event** in one city becomes the **background** for a hundred decisions elsewhere. An **incident** that fills one **chapter** of a report can quietly shape ten years of somebody's ordinary Tuesday.

There are also quieter forces. The **theme** of an age is set long before anybody can name it. A **narrow** group of people with a **prime** interest and a **universal** sense of duty can **establish** what the rest of us call normal. Not always for money. Often just because they could not stand the old way any longer.

The **annual** report will tell you the number of new users. It will not tell you that a small shop two streets away closed because people who used to come in twice a week now **confirm** their orders online and never **prepare** a coffee. A **competitive** market does not **remember** the shop. It only counts what still stands.

If you want to know who actually won, do not read the **output** of the system. Go and look at the empty **shop**, the unused **seat**, the person who did not get the call. That is the **index** nobody keeps, and it is the **crucial** one.

Something **tough** has to happen before any of this becomes visible. A **functional** layer of law, a **frame** of rules, a **root** that holds the rest up. In software the words are borrowed; in public life they are exact. Laws are a **frame** for what people expect, and expectations do the **load**-bearing work long before anyone notices the structure is there.

Sometimes the whole thing does not need a **solid** argument. It needs a **friendly** face and a good **frame** of words. That is how most people get **persuaded**, and most of the time they are perfectly happy to be.

And nobody asks permission to **spin** a story. The **root** of a rumour is always a true fact, seen once, from the wrong angle.

## What the Machine Does to a Household

A tool enters a home slowly, the way weather does. First the television, then the **speaker**, then the small box on the shelf that plays whatever you ask for and does not complain.

Music used to be something you had to own. Now it is air. A **band** from 1968, a **classical** piano sonata, a piece of **folk** singing recorded in a kitchen a long time ago — all of it arrives in a second, and none of it can be held. Some people find that wonderful. My **mom** found it wonderful, and then one day she found a box of her mother's paper photographs and sat down, and the room got very quiet.

If you want to hear something she loved, you have to **compose** the request yourself: title, performer, album, year, and half a minute of luck. Then you wait. Some **remote** corner of the world must wake up and answer, and it does, in less time than you took to decide.

A **studio** is a place where music was once made by people standing near each other. Now it is a **format**, a square on a screen, and the standing-near-each-other has to be invented deliberately. That is the trade. Everything gets easier to reach and harder to touch.

The kitchen tells the same story. There is a **printer** in the corner that has not printed a **page** in four years, and a **dish** machine that fills halfway and complains loudly, and a cutting board so **wooden** it feels like skin when you wash it. And outside the window, on the roof of the **nearby** building, a panel of **solar** cells sits in the sun all day, making electricity out of light, asking nothing for itself.

We **prevent** nothing by buying the newer model. We only replace one thing with a better version of the same thing. The **meat** in the fridge still goes off at the same speed. Nothing about a smart kitchen **exhibit** changes the temperature in the room.

My father still keeps his old phone in a drawer, dead and swollen, because it was the one he used to photograph my mother. He will not throw it out. He says it is not a **luxury** item; it is a **souvenir**, and those are different **categories** of thing.

There is a **gun** shop two doors down from the printer shop, and it has been there longer than I have been alive. On the wall inside, between the two kinds of gun, somebody put a strip of old paper with a line of **poetry** on it. Nobody I asked could tell me who, or why. It was just there, and everybody in the shop seemed to **know** about it, the way you know about a **frame** on a wall without ever being told.

My father once tried to teach me the names of the stars, out of a book, on a roof in summer. He got through four of them and I laughed. He got **hot**, and then quiet. Years later I found the same book and learned the rest by myself, and every time I found a new name I felt a small **appeal** to him, sitting in the next room, not listening.

**Ourselves** is the strange part of all this. We make tools to do better what people already do, and then the tools change us, and then we make tools to repair the change. Nobody **undergoes** this deliberately. It happens the way weather happens.

## The Body Keeps the Receipt

Every technology keeps a bill, and the body pays it. People who spend the **middle** of their working life in front of a screen often find, quite suddenly, that their neck has become a subject of study all by itself.

There is a real **illness** here and it is not mysterious. Hours of stillness, bad light, eyes that never look at anything six metres away. A doctor will call it **nervous** strain, or stress, or simply *the computer*, as if the machine were a character in a story.

Anxiety does not stay in the head. It arrives in the shoulders first, then in the stomach, then in the sleep. Millions of people have learned to feel hungry at the exact moment a message arrives. That is not weakness. It is a nervous system that has been trained, over years, by a machine that wants a response.

The same is true of **awareness**. A watch used to tell the time. Now it counts steps, watches your **nerve** sleep, and gives you a **score** for standing up too late. Some of this is help. **Nevertheless**, it is difficult not to turn every quiet moment into something to correct.

An **examination** by a doctor, an X-ray, a small blood test, a long afternoon in a waiting room: these are slower and duller than a screen, and for exactly that reason they remain reliable. We would trust a scan before we trust a feeling.

On the good days, the machine makes a person feel like the owner of a very large brain. That feeling is real, and it should be taken seriously — but it should also be **regarded** for what it is. A tool that answers instantly makes you poor at waiting. A tool that never forgets makes you poor at forgetting. Both are useful, and both have a price, and the price is paid **outside** the machine.

## Small Pleasures That Nobody Optimized

The best things about this technology are not impressive.

It is the moment when a photograph from nineteen years ago appears in the middle of a conversation, and everyone in the room leans in at once. It is a **fun** piece of software that does one small thing perfectly, for no reason, at no charge.

It is the **sweet** little sound a machine makes when it has finished a job you did not know how to do. It is a **musical** ringtone that used to mean somebody was calling you, now meaning that a **company** wants you to sign up for something.

And it is the strangest of all: a tool doing something **you** did not ask for and did not know was possible, and being so **useful** that you will never again be able to **conclude** that it was only a machine.

And it is the way technology **aid** a person without ever meeting them. A stranger spends three weeks building a small tool, gives it away for nothing, and moves on to something else. There is no name to remember and no **sight** of each other. The **growth** of the whole thing is made almost entirely of people nobody will meet. If I try to **summarize** the twentieth century in one sentence, I **reckon** it is this: a very large number of small, anonymous, careful **actions**, each one pushing a door a little wider, none of them aiming at any particular result. No single action mattered much. The **sum** did.

It is the word a machine gets wrong, because the mistake makes everyone laugh and everyone remembers it forever. Machines are good at being right. They are terrible at being funny on purpose.

Sometimes, late at night, the machine throws a small error message and I feel an **absolute** **burst** of gratitude for it. You know how rare that is? A stupid thing went wrong, and I **sigh**, and I remember that I am talking to a box.

Most of all, it is the way a tool contributes nothing at all. You pick up the phone, and there is nothing new, and you put it down, and the day goes on. Nobody builds a feature for that, and yet that empty minute is where a **pleasant** kind of peace usually lives.

A friend of mine refused every update for three years, just to see what would happen. The machine got slower, the **giant** photo file stopped opening, and she began to describe the software as a creature that needed feeding. **Naturally** she was the last person in the building still using it. She also says it was the happiest three years of her working life.

## Learning to Live With It

Any **device** in the world can be broken by the right person with a thin tool, and any device can be built again. What cannot be rebuilt is a habit.

So here is the **advice** I would give, and I have broken it myself many times: use the machine for the things you cannot do alone, and use your own hands for the things you can. Learn the difference early. Otherwise the **low** hours and the best hours start to look the same, and you will not notice the year you lost.

Do not be afraid of the tools. Be careful with the ones that arrive on their own, the ones that update themselves, the ones that turn every small habit into a **trend** and every small thought into a **subject** worth **tracking**.

I **reckon** there are exactly two ways to end up in front of a screen, and neither of them is a plan. One is that you **bend** toward it, twice a day, for years, because it is easier **than** the other thing. The other is that it happens to you, and you only notice because somebody asks a question you cannot **answer**.

If you do want a rule, take this one. Before you do anything on the machine, decide whether the answer is **available** to someone else, in a **fragment** you would be willing to read out loud. If it is not, that is not a **scare**, that is just a private thing — and private things are the ones worth keeping.

**Due** to a small injury in my right hand, I could not use a mouse for six weeks. Nothing bad happened. I wrote three letters, read one whole book, and learned the names of my neighbours. It was inconvenient **due** to the hand, and it was also the best month of that year.

Do not let the machine become a place where you only **push** things away from you. A screen is very good at that and very bad at the opposite. **Warm** soup, a bad **headache**, a hard conversation with your mother: none of these can be **pushed** into a small square icon, and the moment you try, something **cool** and reasonable happens to your day, and you do not notice what you lost.

One last supplement to the advice, then. If you want to know whether a tool is doing its job, do not ask whether you **use** it more. Ask whether you are **gently** becoming the kind of person who uses it. The tool will not warn you. It will just get **widely** used, and you will be somewhere inside the **category** of people who use it, somewhere in the **mile** between the door and the desk, and the question will have lost its meaning.

A **server** is just a machine that never sleeps. A database is just a very large table. An account is just a promise. A network is just rooms with doors between them. If you understand this, you can use all of it, and you can also walk away from it, and you will know that walking away is a choice and not an accident.

I still keep a paper notebook beside the keyboard. Not because paper is better, but because it is **slow**, and I have **noticed** that I think more clearly when my hand has to **wait**. I do not have a theory for why. I only know that the best note I have ever written was made with a pen that could not check spelling.

There was a **jury** once, in another matter, and one of the men told me the machines had made his job impossible. What he meant was that a picture taken at 3 a.m. could now **capture** a thing no human witness could have survived to describe. I do not think he was right, and I did not **argue**, and I still think about it now.

That is the honest end of it. The tools do not decide what matters. They only decide how fast you can get to the thing, and how little of yourself it costs to go.

That is what this technology has actually given us: not the machines themselves, but the freedom to decide, each day, whether to pick one up.

## A Note on the Word "Usual"

I want to add one thing, which I learned from a woman I once sat beside on a train for four hours and have never seen since. She was carrying a thick notebook of the kind made for accounts, hand-ruled, and she wrote in it with a pencil. When I asked what she was writing, she said she was rewriting a list she had first written at twenty-five, and that she kept it because a screen had once lost her ten years of notes in a single afternoon.

Her complaint was not about any particular company. It was that we have been asked to accept the **usual** machinery as the only machinery, and that a person is not considered normal unless they are logged in, reachable, and accounted for. She found that insulting, and I have stopped calling it unreasonable.

The odd thing about technology is not that it is powerful. It is that it is so confident about the ordinary, and that it has stopped pretending, as older machines did, that it was still new. Everybody used to complain about the machines. Now we simply use them, and nobody says it anymore.
