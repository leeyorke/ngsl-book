# Living in a Society

*人如何被制度、阶层、媒体与信念组织起来，又如何在其中互相亏欠。*

## What Every Society Knows Without Saying

A **society** is not a crowd of people. It is a set of arrangements that nobody wrote down but everybody obeys.

Move to a new city and **within** a week you have learned the local jokes, the rules about who pays for dinner, and which words are polite in this street. This invisible kit is what people call a **culture**.

Every culture answers the same questions in its own way. How do we treat strangers? Who gets the good seat on the train? How much shouting happens before a neighbor calls the police? At first the answers are **tradition** and **custom**. Later they become the water you swim in without noticing.

From the inside, it is hard to **view** your own society clearly. Everything feels **proper** and **easy**, so you cannot see the rules you are obeying. This **matters** a great deal, because the same rule can look fair from one side of the city and unfair from the other.

Sociologists use **various** words to describe the way a society **sorts** its members into groups. They say **social class**, **ethnic** background, **gender**, **race**, age. A **generation** is another strong line. You can change your country and your job, but you cannot change your family or the year you were born.

Each group has its own **organization** — a church, a union, a **club**, a party — and each one teaches the young what to want.

A **single** family may move up or down in ten years. A **current** of money can lift a whole street, or **pull** it under. Society is never still, and that is the point.

## The Line You Are Born Behind

Now we come to the hardest part of the subject: **poverty** and **wealth**.

The **poor** do not all live the same life. Some live in a small town with strong **association** between neighbours. Some live in a big city where nobody knows their name. A **rich** family may have the same blood as a poor one and a **completely** different chance.

We call this a difference in **privilege**. Privilege is not a punishment. It is simply the quiet advantage you get before you start. Good schools are near the houses of the rich. Free clubs with good coaches are cheaper for families who already have money. A **minority** of people get a very large share of the national **wealth**, and the **majority** gets much less.

Look at the number in a newspaper and you will see how strong this is. One family at the top owns a small mountain of money. Ten thousand families at the **bottom** share a dustpan. Any honest **estimate** will show that gap. The **concentration** of money in a few hands is one of the great facts of our time.

What do people do about it? Some ask the state to help. A government runs **welfare**: money, food, health care, and school for those who cannot pay themselves. Some people think this is fair. Others say it takes money from those who work and gives it to those who do not.

**Charity** is the private **version** of the same idea. A **volunteer** gives time, not money. A club raises money for a poor street. A church opens a kitchen in winter. These things are real and they help, but they cannot replace public systems, and the people who run them usually know it.

There is also **bias**. Some people look at a poor family and see a lazy family. Others look at a rich family and see a lucky one. Both views are stories we tell ourselves, not facts.

## Votes, Voices, and Papers

Every few years a **society** makes a big choice together. This is an **election**.

A **candidate** wants your **vote**. The candidate talks about jobs, schools, and price. The candidate tells a story about the past and promises a better future. Then the counting day comes and the winning side takes power.

If the system is healthy, one candidate **wins** by **majority** and the loser accepts the result. If the system is sick, one side takes the office by force and calls it the will of the people. In most countries a final court has the last word on such questions.

In my country the leader of the government is a **president**, but in some countries the same job has another name. In a few countries the **minister** of **justice** is the most powerful person in the room. Titles change; the fight over power does not.

Public **opinion** is stranger than the vote. You can ask ten thousand people a question and get a clear answer. You cannot ask the whole country. Newspapers and television do the asking for us, and so they decide which questions **matter**.

That is the power of **advertisement**. A large company wants to sell one soap. So it buys a prime **channel** and a few minutes of your evening. Twenty seconds of music, a smile, a bottle held up to the light. The product is almost nothing. The feeling is the product.

When the same company runs a **broadcast** that looks like news, the trick is nearly **perfect**. The **newspaper** does something similar in print, and a **magazine** does it with a thin paper and a famous name. A **journalist** who works well knows how to build a scene in three lines.

Where do **ordinary** people talk? In a bar, in a lift, in a school yard, and now on the internet. A **blog** is slower and more personal than a news channel, but it can reach a **listener** ten countries away. A short **chat** at midnight can change what four million people think about a small fire.

The **tone** matters. **Silence** looks like agreement when the rich are quiet and looks like guilt when the poor are quiet. There is no neutral way to report a fact, and readers who know this are the strongest defense a society has.

And if no paper reports a group at all, that group has no **representation**. **Half** the city can disappear from the news in a single week.

## When the Rules Break

A law is a sentence in advance: what you must not do, and what will happen if you do it. When someone breaks the law, we say **crime**. The person who does it is a **criminal**.

Now stand in a **court**. The judge is a government **secretary** of another kind. She reads the facts and says a **sentence** — three years, or a fine, or a warning. If the sentence is a **prison**, the door is often called a **jail**.

Behind that door sit **victims** of three kinds. There is the person who was hurt directly. There is the family. There is the whole community, which now lives slightly less safely. When we weigh these three, we must judge the act, not the crowd.

Justice has a bad habit of arriving **slowly**. A rich man can pay a good lawyer and walk away. A poor man cannot. A young **minor** from a poor street waits three years for a case that takes six months. This is why some people become **hero** figures in the press and others never appear at all.

Some lawyers spend their lives on the other side. They **advocate** for people who cannot speak for themselves. A good judge admits when the system has **failed** and admits it plainly.

Every justice system has a **judgment** inside it that is not written down: who is believed, and who is not. Reading that judgment is one of the hardest skills in a society.

## War, Peace, and the People Who Refuse

The heaviest word in this topic is **war**.

An **army** is a large group of people trained to use force. Its job is not to protect a single person. Its job is to **serve** a **politics** of the whole country, and that politics may be wise or cruel.

When a war breaks out, the **media** often supports it. The **social** mood changes in a week. Yesterday's careful questions about the soldiers disappear. Tomorrow's enemies are usually poor, far away, and easy to hate.

A **soldier** can lose **battle** after battle and still believe deeply in the cause. A single **troop** can cross a river at night and be hungry and wet and still keep moving. War lives on **labor**, on factories, on fuel, and mostly on the young.

Some people never accept the story. They **march** in the street with signs. They want **peace**, not victory. A **strike** is the same idea with a different tool: workers stop working, and the whole society suddenly **notices** their pay.

Some people refuse more completely. They walk across a border with one bag and become a **refugee**. Some are **violent** and are called a **terrorist**. The word is used **carefully** by honest people and carelessly by others, which is itself worth watching.

In a healthy society the difficult ones are protected too. That is the whole test: a **society** that protects the weak, the strange, and the angry is stronger, not weaker. A famous **hero** of civil courage is usually a person who knew the crowd was watching and did the right thing anyway.

## Faith, Church, and the Older Generation

A **religion** is a set of answers to the largest questions. Why am I here? What happens after I die? What is good?

A **church**, a temple, or a meeting room is where a **belief** becomes a **faith** that you share with other people. A **god** or something larger is at the center of it. A **minister** leads the music and the talk. **Religious** argument in politics is often honest and is sometimes a **cover** for something else.

Culture, religion, and tradition are hard to separate. One often holds up the other. A country may protect an old **custom** and protect a **freedom** in the same week, and both decisions arrive dressed in the same language.

A song or a **poem** of three lines can carry a value across a whole **generation**. A **secret** kept for forty years is a quiet kind of **union** between people who can never say it aloud.

## Work, Youth, and Ordinary Days

A **society** is also a set of jobs.

In the morning an **operator** starts a machine. A **secretary** answers a phone. A **senior** manager reads a long report and signs it. The technique is old, but the tools are new. The same worker in another country may do the same job with a better machine.

**Enterprise** is the word for a business someone builds. Small **enterprise** creates most new jobs in most countries. A **business** owner takes the risk, and the risk is real.

Now look at the level below the worker. **Unemployment** is the number of people who want a job and cannot find one. In a bad year that number **soars** and stays high for years, because an **enterprise** that closes does not reopen by itself.

Industry runs on **steel**, on **concrete**, on a supply of parts and fuel. If the **supply** chain breaks, the price at the shop goes up in a week, and the news becomes a **chart** with a line going the **wrong** way.

Youth feels all of this first. A young person needs a **desk**, a room, and a chance to fail a little. A **secretary** job at twenty can teach more than a title at thirty.

Life has other rhythms. **Leap** years. **Cancer** and care. **Pregnancy** and the first long night. **Psychological** health and the slow work of **therapy**. A **magic** trick at a **birthday** party. A **grin** across a table.

It is easy to forget that all of this happens inside a society. A **generation** that believes its life is unusual is usually wrong about that.

## How a Society Is Built, One Thing at a Time

Nobody designs a society in one afternoon. It is built out of small **adjustments** and ordinary habits, by people who push, **adapt**, and argue.

Newspapers print opinions. Readers disagree. Somebody **accuses** the paper of bias, and the paper **responds**. Both sides get a little wrong, both sides get a little right, and next week the argument continues. That is not failure. That is the machine working.

Where you are born will still matter more than it should. Being **able** to read, being **able** to stay healthy, being within a short walk of a good school — these decide much of your life before you do anything.

The best **technique** for living together is not clever. It is **precisely** dull: keep the rules, keep talking, help the poor, punish the criminal, let the court decide, let the vote decide, and never confuse a loud **opinion** with the truth.

Nobody has the **determination** for this every day. There are bad weeks. A friend will **disappoint** you, a company will close, a party will win and act **badly**. You rest, and then you go back to the ordinary work.

Community is a kind of **cooperation** that must be rebuilt every day, because it decays every day. **Bar** the door against cruelty. Push a little **steel** beam into place. Add one **brick** where the wall is thin. The **chip** in the paint is not the wall. Neither is the **loose** argument at dinner. That is where you start.

The **hero** is the person who knows all this and stays **calm** on an ordinary Tuesday. The **poem** is small. The **mood** is mostly steady. The **soul** of a society is not its loudest day but its **average** one, and that is built, day by day, by people who will never make a **title** or a **chart** for it.

If you want a fuller picture, read also [[The Shape of a City]] and [[How Work Actually Works]].

## The People Behind the Numbers

A number can hide a person, and a person can hide a number. It is worth doing the work in both directions, because the two together are what we mean by society.

Take one street. My **dad** grew up on it, and he is quick to **emphasize** that it was nothing special: nineteen houses, a shop, and a chip shop that changed hands twice. What he describes **primarily** as dull was in fact a small working economy. The neighbours lent each other tools, which meant a ladder cost the whole street almost nothing. The women organized a **pool** of minders for the school run, and my mother learned two new trades at a **voluntary** evening class in a church hall. People said the class was harmless. It was **indeed** harmless, and it was also the first time anybody in my family read a **literary** book out loud, to much **laughter**, and then in silence.

There was a football **league**, and there was a **boom** of front rooms, so each house extended a foot every decade. My father spent thirty years in a factory that built seats for **vehicles**. He liked the joke: he had made something to sit on, and it had gone nowhere.

The **currency** of that street was not money. It was trust, and trust was hard to tax.

Now take a second life, because a society is a crowd of these. A young woman at nineteen, living in a shared flat, decides to tell her colleagues that she is **gay**. The moment arrives on a Thursday, in the kitchen, with a **cigarette** burning on the sill and nothing dramatic happening at all. Nobody leaves. The conversation moves on, and this — the absence of an event — is the achievement. It took forty years and a great many unhappy people to reach that Thursday, and the **attitude** that made it possible was built **especially** in workplaces and **cultural** places — anywhere people are forced to be near each other.

Her brother's partner is **pregnant**, and the **interior** of their flat has become the whole of geography. The bus runs **frequently** enough to trust and **rarely** enough to plan around. When it is late she will **grab** her coat and walk, and he will **kick** himself for not checking the timetable. Their plans are **flexible** now in theory and quite fixed in practice.

Meanwhile the town enters a **revolution** nobody voted for. A plant closes; the shop with the ladder shuts; a **tip** opens on the old sports field. A society asks its people to **commit** to a place that no longer **corresponds** to what it was. Some **survive** by leaving. Others stay and **seek** a way to build something out of a **stable** life that never quite arrived. A few are asked to be **mature** about it, to absorb the loss without complaint, and to be courteous about it as well — and to do so **whereas** their neighbours in the next town are paid to sit still. A **survival** strategy made of manners is still a strategy, and it is the only one most people have. It is never **smooth**. It is simply the only one available.

Crime there is not a grand **attack** on the state. It is a broken window, a coat lifted from a hook, a **potentially** violent young man from a good family in a **highly** paid job who still cannot resist a small **vice**, and a girl who steals bread and tells the court, with a **tear** in her voice, that she has no regret — and she is right. The judge looks at the file. There is not a **hint** in it of the person behind the accusation, and that is the failure.

Two habits would help. First, we should **generate** public spaces where people can **react** to each other without a fee — a park, a free pool, a **pop** concert in a school yard. Second, we should **inspire** people to have **input** before the decision, not after. A law made without them is a law that survives **potential** **opposition** only by luck.

What we are defending is not a flag. It is the **convention** that everyone inside a country deserves the same protection **versus** the same danger. That convention has done more to stop **racial** abuse than any number of good intentions, and it stays worth **revealing**, one honest person at a time.
