# The Long Way There

*一次放弃打折机票、改乘慢车与渡轮的旅行，路上遇见的人，以及后来写成的那篇稿子。*

## The Ticket

For years I believed that a journey began the moment you bought the **ticket**. That sounds foolish in print, but I grew up somewhere where distance was the only thing worth measuring, and the cheapest seat was always the right one. My employer was a large **institution**: a **committee** of directors, a floor of **management** who spoke on the phone all day and never once looked out of the window. They covered the trip, so the **fee** felt like somebody else's problem. I filled in the form, read the **instruction** printed on the back, and pressed the button. Somewhere in a database a machine gave me a **claim** number, as if the whole business were a small and temporary illness.

Air travel had become so ordinary that the word had lost its **whole** weight. You **fly**, you sleep, you land, and nothing happens to you. What I wanted that winter was a slower **experience**. An **editor** I had worked with asked me to write about the art of going nowhere. "**Please**," she said, and she never said please to anyone, "take the long way, and tell me what you find."

I opened the **map**. The short route was a **flight** from the capital, a bus, a taxi — six hours door to door, an entire day saved. The long route was a night on a **rail** line to the coast, two days on a **ship**, and then, because the ferry had been **cancel**led for engine trouble, a small **boat** belonging to a man named Seamus. He was a **veteran** of that crossing. Looking at the water, he told me that the sea takes a wise man's judgment along with a fool's, and that I should not stand up on the deck for the first hour.

I cancelled the flight. In a **foreign** language the word is heavier than in mine, and I felt it in my stomach for days. Then I bought the train ticket, packed one bag, and told my mother I would be gone for five weeks. She said, "Then write to me every day." It was a **constraint** I had not expected and could not argue with. Five weeks, in the **term** she used, meant a long time.

## The Night Train

The **airline** desk in my own city had offered me a seat beside a **member** of a small travelling committee of engineers, all of them bound for the same **international** conference. I told them my plans had changed. "Changed?" said the woman at the desk. "**Cancel**led?" I said yes. "Then I will **remove** your name from the list," she said, and did it, and looked pleased with herself, as if a system had been tidied.

I had **hired** a taxi for the station three days earlier and cancelled that too, which is the **general** way of travel for a man who cannot decide. The **platform** at the central station was a long low roof with a hundred people standing under it in the wrong shoes. Above it the sky was the pale **pink** of a thing that has not decided whether it is morning.

Night trains are strange. The **arrival** time is printed on your ticket in a language you can read, and for eleven hours the world outside the window is mostly your own reflection. I slept badly and woke at the border.

There were two queues at the border, and only one of them was moving. The **immigration** officer was a young man who had learned English from a **writer** nobody there had ever read, and he asked what I did. I said I was a writer. "Then you will not like this country," he said, and stamped the page, and waved me through. It was the kindest warning I have ever received. He stamped with such **style**, too, one quick movement, as if passport control were a performance he had perfected. His uniform was newly pressed and the badge on it was **gold**, and it seemed to **represent** something about the state that mattered very much to him. He asked where I was going, and I said **overseas**, and he **surprisingly** asked the same question twice, as if the word might **operate** differently the second time.

An **aircraft** went over very low, so low that everyone on the platform looked up. Nobody said anything. In my country an aircraft that low is a warning. Here it was ordinary, and the ordinariness **shocked** me more than the plane. The **significance** of a thing, I learned that night, is not a property of the thing. It is a **relation** between the thing and the person looking at it.

## Signs and Slow Water

By the second day I had a **guide**, a young man called Ferdi who had driven a taxi for eleven years and knew the coast the way I know my own street. **Tourism** had arrived before me. There was a **tourist** office with a plastic model of the harbour, a row of **visitor** centres, and a **tour** of the old fort that ran three times a day in four languages. The town had exactly one **attraction**, and eleven agencies were competing to sell it to you. The local **survey** office, next door, offered a free **map** of the walking routes, which I still **hang** up in my flat.

I bought the first ticket. Our **guide** pointed out a **photograph** in the entrance hall from 1911 and asked whether I knew who was in it. I did not. He told me it was a ship, and that his grandmother had worked in the hotel behind it, and then he stopped talking. This is the **general** shape of a conversation here: a fact, and then a small door, and then quiet.

My **camera** failed on the third day, and Ferdi lent me his. It was heavier than mine and made a **click** like a coin dropping. In the pictures that came back I looked like a **fellow** passenger on somebody else's holiday. It **amazed** me how quickly a place can become a habit. I complained about the film, and he laughed and said, in English, "Every place has a broken camera. That is the **characteristic** of a place." Then he added, **moreover**, that my camera was not broken, only **reluctant**, which I have thought about a lot since.

The coast here does not **relate** to the sea the way a coast is supposed to. It **left** the town and turned inland, and the sea came up and took the town instead. A **hill** of grey stone stands where the harbour should be. Along the **shore** the water is **raw** green, cold enough to hurt, and small fishing **vessels** come in at dusk with their lights on early. I ate **raw** fish that night, and a **grain** of salt sat on my **lip** for an hour, and I did not mind it at all.

On the **beach** below the old fort I met an old **immigrant** woman whose family had come here three generations ago, from a country I had never heard of, and who spoke a **mixture** of two languages that I could not **match** to any I knew. She asked where I was from, and I said my country, and she said, "**Abroad**?" as if the word itself were a joke. I became, for the length of an evening, her **ally** in an argument I did not fully understand. She then talked for forty minutes without pause, and I understood perhaps a fifth of it, and it was the most **funny** and most **moving** conversation of my life. She gave me a **package** of dried fish wrapped in paper, and refused money for it, and then **insured** me, in the tone of a mother, that I would eat it tonight and not tomorrow. She also **mailed** me a card every month for a year, and each one began with the same **aggressive** joke about my handwriting. I ate the fish that night, standing in the cold, and the **cry** of the gulls around me was **extremely** loud.

## The Way Through

We **sailed** the next morning at four, and I was sick, and the sun came up anyway. By ten o'clock we were past the last headland and there was nothing in front of us but **ocean** and **depth**, an **adventure** in the old-fashioned sense of the word, meaning mostly that I was cold and bored. There is a **wide** kind of calm that comes from having nothing to look at and nothing to decide. Your **pace** slows and then your thinking slows with it, and after a while you can **perceive** things you were too busy to notice on land: the exact **figure** of the horizon, the way a **golf** course on a distant island looks like a stain, the fact that the **power** of the sun on water changes every twenty minutes and nobody notices. The **source** of all that light, I decided, must **remain** a question for later.

We landed at a town with no reason to exist except a **historic** church and a **forest**. I had booked a room over a **pub** where a woman played a **guitar** and a **singer** sang badly and well, in that order. Over the second beer a man explained that the local football **tournament** had been cancelled, and then the whole pub **complained** about it for an hour. I understood perhaps a third of the words. It was the best hour of the trip. There is a **tension** in a room full of strangers who are all complaining in the same language, and the tension is not bad. It is the sound of a **symbol** everyone understands without needing a definition. The whole evening **shifted**, at about eleven, into singing, and nobody decided it and nobody could have stopped it.

The road inland went **alongside** a river, up through a **valley** where the rock **formation** on the left looked like a wave that had stopped. Two villages sit three miles **apart**, and between them a path **split** in two and I took the wrong one, and walked an hour before I could **distinguish** between being lost and simply being new. At a farm I asked for water and the farmer's daughter carried it up in a plastic pail, and I drank from it and it tasted of **bone**. She watched me with **close** attention and I was **afraid**, for no reason, to say thank you too quickly, as if she might read it. I have **subsequently** learned, from a letter, that she had an **attachment** to a brother in another country, which is probably where the first question came from.

Above us was a **mountain** with snow on it in **spring**, and below the path a **reserve** full of deer who did not move when I passed. The **desert** on the far side of the ridge looked **mainly** like a sand-coloured lake, and I walked three **inches** of it and then sat down in the heat. My own **fear**, which had been running quietly under everything since the airport, finally **relaxed**. Not gone. Just **relaxed**. I think a strange place **eases** that feeling in most people, and I do not know why.

## The Long Way There

It took five days to reach the town at the end, not five hours, and I had a **tent**, a sleeping bag, a camp stove, and a **mobile** with no signal. I had a paper **map** and a paper list of names. And I had **insurance**, which does not, I now know, cover a man who takes the wrong path uphill in the rain for the **experience** of it. My old peers at the office would have called that a decision to avoid a decision, and **ultimately** they would not have been wrong. The **facility** I had actually paid for was a single room with a hard bed, and I slept in it for one night out of politeness.

The town was a **settlement** of about eight hundred people, a school, a clinic, two shops, and a committee room with a broken **electric** light. The mountains **surround** it on three sides. An old woman with an **innocent** face explained, carefully, that two families had **borrowed** land from each other for forty years without a paper between them, and that a lawyer from the capital had called this, in a **technical** tone, "a customary arrangement." She said the word **ownership** as if it were a foreign object she had picked up with two fingers. Nobody in the valley would **stir** themselves to claim the land officially. It would require a **sanctioned** form from the capital, a fee, and a wait of months that **constantly** grow longer, for a plot of **personally** useless grass.

There was no **democracy** in the valley in any form I recognised. One **voter** per household, in theory. The **chamber** in the capital decided things, and the **counsel** who advised them was the only person I met who stated things clearly, with **precision**, as if precision were a kind of kindness. The real **constraint** was water, not law, and the water belonged to a company from another country. The plan was a quarry. The company would **guarantee** jobs for twenty years, and the local council had agreed to a compensation **settlement** that **amounted** to almost nothing. In return, a mountain the size of a small island would be removed from the map. The wood, the oil, the grain, everything there went straight out to be **export**ted; the machines and the fuel would be **import**ted. The company had one **member** on the council and had **ban**ned the discussion from the local paper. Someone had **imposed** a tax on anyone who wrote a letter about it.

The company's **regional** office sent a **specialized** team to the village to **educate** people about the benefit. Their presentation consisted mainly of a slide show about how many trucks a year the mountain could carry, and a question period in which nobody spoke. Their **preference** was clearly for people who could not read. I stayed nine days, and I did not want to. I went with a **fellow** passenger from the capital, a young **actor** who was playing a doctor in a play about the valley, and a **plot** simpler than the truth. He asked me every night what the "real" story was, and I told him, and I told him again, and by the third night I understood that I had **split** the story into pieces to keep control of it. A story without a person in it is not a story. It is a **report**. And a report can be right and still **fail** to describe anything. I wanted to **engage** with the man, and in the end I could not, and the word **guilty** sat on me for the rest of the night.

## The Article I Could Not Print

I wrote eleven thousand words. I was careful. I checked every name, every figure, every date, the **term** of the original permit, the amount of compensation offered, the two prior occasions on which the same company had promised the same things. I **undertake** to say that I did this well, because the doing of it was the one part I was good at. I **undertook** to show the manager of the quarry a copy before I printed it, and he agreed, and he read it in my presence, and he said that my description of the negotiations was "too general to be fair." That was fair, and I made it more **specific**, and I **specify**ied the date of the second meeting, and I gave him the **explanation** he had given the council, in his own words, in full, without a surprise. Then I sent the final text to my editor.

She rang at eleven at night. Her voice was strange, half astonished, half apologetic. The company had a lawyer in the capital, and the story was no longer a story. She had never **rejected** a piece outright in eleven years, and she said the word **critical** with real pain. She wanted to send a long **reply** to the lawyer first, and to copy me in, **despite** my advice, which I have forgiven her for. She would **replace** the whole second half of the piece, she said, or she would **inform** the lawyer that the paper stood by every word of it. The **critic** who reviewed my earlier work called the piece "an **intellectual** fantasy with a **motivation** that could be admired but not believed." The **reporter** who helped me with the documents said it was a **misreading** of how the valley works, and that I had **confused** myself with a set of rules that are not written anywhere. Both were wrong, and I could prove it, and it did not **matter**, which was the most depressing thing I have ever learned. A guarantee of a fair hearing is the most **significant** thing a small paper can offer, and mine had quietly lost the ability to make it. What I **took** from that, in the **end**, was **limited** but not small: I had been **mistaken** about one date, and the date **determined** nothing, and yet the whole thing fell. Truth is necessary, but truth is not sufficient. A story needs a person in it to **satisfy** anybody, and mine had been reduced to a set of numbers. I had been taught the first half of a sentence for **years** and nobody had ever **mentioned** the second half.

The law that week required clinical and **biological** details to be reviewed before publication, so the piece was delayed for a month. The doctor who had led me through the **treatment** rooms said the law was sensible and I should **respect** it, and then he **paused**, and told me that a colleague had lost her post for arguing the other way, and that the **legislation** concerning **abortion** in the capital was the only reason women from this valley were not travelling two hundred kilometres, and that **medical** staff there worked in **constant** fear of the law. I wrote all of that down. I did not use any of it. I told the editor it was not relevant to a story about a quarry, and she said, "I know," and neither of us said anything else, and I went home. Whether that was the right decision or a lazy one, I still do not know. I would like to be able to say that I do know. I **prefer** to believe I was frightened, which is cleaner, but I was frightened, and I was also lazy, and the two are neighbors. They sit on the same shelf in me, and they often **arrive** together.

I resigned from the paper the next spring. Not in protest. I simply could not sit in the meetings and be the person who had proposed the piece. My standing in the trade was more or less **finished**. A few people wrote kindly. A fellow editor asked me to teach a course on reporting, which I agree to do, and which is a strange way to keep a **skill** alive. I gratefully accepted. In the first class I asked the students to describe a street they know, and it took them **forever**, which was, I think, the point.

## What I Did Not Expect

I did not expect the **pleasure** of travel to be the **opposite** of what I had always assumed. I thought the pleasure was in going somewhere. It is not. The pleasure is in the space between where you are and where you are expected to be — the interval of the crossing itself, the hours when you are **absent** from your own life and no one can **reach** you. I did not expect the day to be about absorption and not about attainment.

I did not expect the transaction with the people I met to be so clean. You show up, you are hungry, they feed you, you talk, you pay. A fee for lodging, a fee for the photograph, a fee for the coffee that is included and should not be. Nobody in the valley was performing for me. They were just people, which is a kind of **loveliness** nobody had mentioned to me. They did not need me to **resist** anything or to **dedicate** anything. They wanted nothing **particularly**, which was, for somebody like me, a small mercy. I have since **blessed** them, quietly, in the way you **bless** a stranger who gives you water.

I did not expect, on the last morning, to be unable to say goodbye to a single one of them. There was a **complaint** to make, and it **amazed** me that I could not make it. Some **anxiety**, some **sorrow**, would have been **lovely**. What I felt instead was a very **strange** peace, and a **symbol** of it: a rope of light across the water, so **newly** made that it had not yet learned to be a rope. A **transition**, not an ending. A **gap** rather than a wall.

I have recovered from that. I still take the slow way. I have learned that a return ticket is not a promise but an option, and that the road alongside the coast is not a shorter version of the sea, it is a different **medium** entirely. And I have learned that the world is full of wise people who will help you for nothing, and that if you contribute a little of your own **attention**, they will help you a **great** deal more than you anticipate and more than the cost. Their contribution is never written on a receipt.

It is a small thing, and it sounds small, but I have **finally** found the **adult** way to say it. I had to **adjust** my **focus** on the whole question, therefore, and to **exclude** the words I wanted to use, and to say only what I could **necessarily** defend. The long way is the way. Everything else is a slight on the map, and the slight is what you pay to avoid the obvious fact that you were never going to see the thing if you took the short cut. I gave the whole of that summer to a hotel bill, and it bought me the rest of my life in one piece. I would certainly do it again. But I definitely would not call it a holiday now. It was not a holiday. It was a **residence**, and I resided in it, and the fact of that is the only thing I have.

**Thank you** for the good question. You are the first person to ask me this, and I **enjoyed** the answer more than the journey, which is strange for a man who spent five weeks on a train to say. I expect to explain it to you again. I hope you will ask. I will pretend I have not told you, and I will tell you, and the old story will be slightly different, and that is why we travel — so that the story is alive, and the road keeps moving, and the arrival never quite arrives, and the ticket in your hand is not a pass but an **argument** that the world is bigger than the routine that made it.
