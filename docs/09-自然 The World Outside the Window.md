# The World Outside the Window

关于自然的一篇长文：从窗外的天气讲到脚下的土地、身边的动物，以及我们怎样给这些不会说话的东西命名。

## The Window That Faces North

There is a window in my kitchen that faces north, and for twenty years it has shown me the same four things: a **cloud** crossing, a **bird** **landing**, **weather** arriving, and weather leaving. My grandmother hung a barometer on the wall beside it, a wooden box with a brass face and a needle that moves like a slow hand. When the needle fell, she would say the weather was thinking about **rain**, and she was right often enough that the habit outlived her.

A barometer is a strange little **tool**. It does not tell you what will happen. It tells you what the air is doing right now, which is a different and in some ways more useful thing. My grandmother's **forecast** was built out of four signals: the color of the **sky**, the smell of the ground, the sitting of the hens, and the **dog**. That dog could feel a pressure drop before the glass moved. She would say the **wind** had changed its mind, and she was right more often than not, and I have never once caught her saying it without going to the window to check.

Windows are honest instruments. They are also badly placed. Mine is fixed, and I cannot adjust the angle to follow the light, and in summer the afternoon sun hits the glass and turns the whole room into an oven. In winter the same glass goes **dark** at four o'clock and the yard becomes a **deep** blue nothing, and I sit there with a lamp on, watching my own face. Everything I know about the seasons, I know from a gap in a wall.

What arrives through that gap never stops surprising me. Rain is a sound before it is **anything** else — a ticking on the glass, then a hiss, then a gray curtain that hides the far hedge completely. In a **storm** the same rain arrives sideways, and the noise changes pitch, and a **flash** of light turns the garden white for half a second and prints an afterimage on my eyes. After a storm everything is **fresh**. The air is washed, the dark corners of the yard smell like a cellar, and there is a particular quality of light that lasts about twenty minutes and then goes.

Winter is a different country. **Snow** changes the acoustics of everything, because snow absorbs sound, and the world outside becomes a library. Footsteps go soft. A car half a mile away arrives as a faint hiss, and if the cold is **extreme** enough you can hear it before you see it. Snow also changes the **line** of the world. A fence that was a fence in October is a row of small bumps in January, and the field beyond it is not a field at all but one enormous white surface with a **tall** tree standing in it, and that tree looks like a punctuation **mark** in a sentence nobody is writing.

Sometimes the cold is just a fact and not a beauty. Four days of hard frost, a **crack** running the length of the road, a **slip** of ice on every step, a **hell** of a morning where nothing moves until eleven. The **absence** of leaves is not sad exactly, but it is an honest kind of loss, and the trees wear it the way people wear a long coat. Then one morning in late February a **yellow** primrose opens by the wall, and the whole long winter is suddenly a thing that ended.

I stopped **pretending** the window is scenery a long time ago. It is equipment, like the barometer, and the two together give me a **unit** of measurement I can **actually** use: not numbers, but a sense of whether the day is about to change. I **recall** my grandmother saying it in exactly one way. She said: watch the **upper** sky, because that is where the weather decides things. She was not a scientist. She had a flash of insight on a schedule, and the insight was always about the upper air, and she was right.

The sky, it turns out, is one **phase** of the water cycle, and everything above the window is water in a different form, circling in a loop we never see the bottom of. The word for that loop is not mysterious, it is just long, and most of us learn it once and never think about it again. What makes the sky interesting is not the physics. It is that the sky is the one part of the natural world that **acts** on a schedule we cannot argue with, and that we watch anyway.

## Water, Land, and What We Take From It

The land I was raised on is not dramatic. It is a shallow valley, four miles wide, with a **river** at the bottom of it that everyone in the family has always called "the **stream**" and that is actually too wide and too deep for that word. It is the sort of river that looks slow and is not. Watch it long enough and you will see the water is not moving forward at all — it is turning in place, folding back on **itself**, wearing the same bend again and again, the whole surface a **flow** that goes nowhere while the water acts as if it is going somewhere.

Nobody taught me that. I worked it out by sitting on the bank, which is a thing children do that adults have forgotten how to do. Adults keep moving. They have a destination. A child on a bank has no destination and so can see things.

The river made a **lake** once, in my grandmother's time, when the valley was smaller and the rain was heavier. The lake is gone now, drained and farmed, but there is a dip in the field where it was, and in a wet year water stands in that dip and the dip again becomes a small lake, and the whole thing is a **structure** repeating itself on a scale of about ninety years. The land itself has a memory, and its memory is slow, and its memory is written in the shape of water.

A **rock** is the other thing children learn early and badly. I grew up with the idea that rocks were dead and therefore boring and I held that idea for about thirty years. Then someone put a **stone** in my hand that was not a stone at all but a fossil, the shape of a shell, the shell of an **animal** that had lived and died and been buried and had its bones turned to rock in a process so slow that the word "**gradually**" is useless for it. The animal itself is gone. Its outline is not. Somebody sitting in a dark room can look at a broken **piece** of gray rock and see an animal's ribs, and that is a very strange thing to be holding.

Watch the river long enough and you start to see what it carries. A large **proportion** of what is in that water arrived there as **sand**, and the sand came off a field, and the field got the sand off a hill, and the hill got it off a mountain. Nothing is ever really in one place. Things are only ever in transit, and we are all made of the same transit.

The valley is also, of course, useful, which is the part that gets less poetic. Land is a **resource**, and like all resources it is a **stake**, and the interesting question is never whether the land is valuable but who the value belongs to. The soil in that valley is good. It is good because four hundred years of small decisions — where to put a hedge, when to leave a field fallow, whether to keep a hedgerow or a fence — added up to something that looks **similar** to what is there now and is actually nothing alike. The same field in another country grows less. Nobody has a **quick** explanation.

The **economy** of that valley used to be an **environmental** question in the plain old sense. It was about grass and rain and where the water went. Now it is a transportation question, which is the same question wearing different clothes. The **global** market does not care what the soil is doing. It cares what the freight costs. The distance between those two facts is the whole of the modern environmental problem in one sentence, and I do not know how to make that sentence less depressing.

**Climate** is where this stops being local, though the effects are entirely local. A **dispute** over a new reservoir on the river **near** us ran for six years and **involved** three **institutional** bodies, and the whole thing turned out to be about the **capacity** of the dam and the **probability** of one flood every hundred years. That is what a climate argument looks like up close: four honest engineers disagreeing about a number. Abstract nonsense, in other words, wearing a tie.

But the river is a **vital** thing, and I want to say clearly that the local argument is the only one that has ever changed anything. Nobody **approves** a stream **protection** rule in a global conference. Somebody approves it in a hall, in a committee room, in a building with a bad heating system, over a long afternoon, because forty people who care about a river are in a room. The **administration** that runs that hall is not impressive from the inside, and I have met **maybe** three of its **personnel**, and two of them were extremely good at their jobs. That is the actually interesting thing about nature: it is entirely local in its effects and entirely **connected** in its causes, and the gap between those two facts is where all the work is.

A person in that hall once said something I have carried around for years. He said: we do not have the **reality** of the **damage**, we have the **expectation** of it, and the gap between those two is where bad decisions come from. He was right, and the **importance** of that sentence has not faded, and I still **owe** him for saying it in a meeting that eleven people did not find worth **staying** for.

## The Work of Growing Things

A **crop** is the most honest object I know. You can see the whole argument in a field. Some weeks the plants are a **thick** green and everything is fine. Then a week of rain you did not want, then a crack of heat, and the leaves curl in the afternoon and the field looks like it is trying to hide from you. The farmer does the same thing the river does. He does not argue with it. He **searches** for another answer. He plants a different variety, or he plants in a different week, or he **accepts** the loss and works out what it cost him, and by the end of the year he has a number and an **overall** sense of whether the year was worth it.

That is what farming actually is underneath the poetry: an ongoing **effort** to keep a good guess from becoming a bad year. A good year is a good guess. A bad year is a bad guess. The man is the same in both, and if you watch him for one season you can see that, and it is one of the more **brilliant** things about human beings that they keep guessing in the same direction for four hundred years.

A field is also a **private** document. Every hedge is a sentence in a language we have mostly stopped reading, and a farm is a library of those sentences. When two brothers divide an **estate**, the division goes into a lawyer's office and comes out as lines on a map, and those lines are arbitrary in the way all human lines are arbitrary, and then the hedges and the trees grow up along them, and in eighty years the line stops looking arbitrary at all, because the land has agreed to it. That is a strange kind of **moral** lesson and I think about it more than is probably healthy.

The **environment** of a working field is a serious thing and I do not say that lightly. There is a **leg** of that fence which has been replaced six times. There is a corner where the soil is a different color because a different person farmed it, and you can see it from the road, and it is the most obvious history lesson in the whole county. Nobody put a sign up. The crop itself is the sign, and it has been the sign for two centuries.

The hedge in front of my house is **effectively** a wall made of hawthorn, and it is a job like any other, and I do it badly and twice a year, and I have come to think that a thing which must be **maintained** is not a thing you own, it is a thing you are borrowing. That reframe did not reduce the work. It reduced the guilt, which is a fair exchange.

The old gates are **iron** and they rust. I have one on my own place, and every year I tell myself I will paint it and every year I do not, and every year it loses a little more of itself. My neighbor says I would rather write about the gate than close it. She is **correct**, and I have stopped **denying** it, and she has stopped needling me, which was the real **resolution**.

I do not want to be sentimental about any of this. The romance of the small farm is mostly a story that farmers tell each other in bad weather. But I have a debt to a set of people I never met, and the debt is paid in beans and beef and bread, and it would be a strange thing to not pay it.

## The Company of Animals

The dog in this story is my grandmother's dog, and I have already used him twice as an instrument, which is a way of not saying that I loved him. He was a **famous** dog in the village, mostly for a specific and unappealing talent involving bins, and for the way he attached himself to anyone **carrying** a bag. He did not need training for that. It was a natural **feature** of the breed, and it got him a name that stuck, and the name stuck harder than the talent **deserved**.

Animals are not simpler than people. They are only more direct. A dog **expresses** a whole evening with one look at a doorway. A **horse** can tell you the mood of a field before any human in it can. The **cat** in my neighbor's barn has a **status** in that barn that no person could get in a lifetime, and everyone knows it, and nobody discusses it, and that silence is itself a kind of institutional arrangement.

**Wild** animals are harder to see and much easier to feel. Go into a wood in the morning and you will notice the whole place is working. A bird builds a nest that takes six days, and a **baby** bird in that nest is a ridiculous thing, all head and no neck and **absolutely** no dignity. A **black**bird sings for ten hours a night, and the whole wood is full of that sound by four in the morning, and I assume he does not know any of this is a performance, and I find it complicated to believe he does not enjoy it either. A prey animal sees predators because it is built to, and the thing we call instinct is really an entire nervous system organized around one question: is that shape something I need to know about?

The word for a creature's range is its **territory**, and the word for the border of it is a **boundary**, and the whole arrangement is a set of agreements made entirely without language. How far does a fox go? Where does the badger stop? It is not a law, it is a consensus, and a consensus requires a great deal of **mutual recognition**, which means every animal must be able to identify not only who lives here but who is looking. An animal that cannot be identified is not a neighbor, it is a threat, and the whole social structure of a wood rests on the fact that everybody knows everybody, **themselves** included.

This is why a fence is such an interesting object. A fence is a human sentence about where a territory ends, and it works on about sixty percent of the animals that meet it. The rest **ignore** it or step around it, and the ones that step around it are usually the ones that treat the fence as a **significant** event in their lives, which is a funnier outcome than people expect. And every so often a fence is **merely** a fence, and a wall is merely a wall, and the whole animal world is walking past a hunk of wood and nails and has not once stopped to wonder what it means.

The one thing no wild animal ever does is compete on purpose. They compete because everything is a resource and nothing is a gift, and a horse in a field will not move for a dog and a dog in a yard will not move for a horse, and this is not cruelty, it is just a system of priorities that is very efficient and completely without any sense of fairness. An animal that could not handle a **competitor** would not be here.

And there is a cost to all of this that we do not like to say out loud. Every animal living in a valley like this is living in a place we have altered. The hedge dates from 1800-something, the field boundary dates from 1890, the river was moved in 1962 to make room for a road. The whole thing is a complicated history of human decisions, and the wildlife is not living in nature. The wildlife is living in our leftovers, and we keep the leftovers and call it a nature reserve, and that is not a **criticism**, it is just what it is.

A heron stands in the shallows one morning a week and does not move for forty minutes, and I do not move either. It is the closest thing I have to a religious experience, and I am not a religious person, and I have stopped trying to **convert** the feeling into something I am allowed to call by that name.

## Names, **Nouns**, and the Weather

My grandmother's notebook is the object I want to talk about, and I have thought about it for years. It is small and the cover is well worn and it is full of notes in a hand that gets worse as the pages go on, and I have read it more than I have read most books.

The first rule in that notebook is that the noun comes before the feeling. Awe is not a species. Write *song thrush*, and then write what it did to you. My grandmother wrote *wren*, and then, on the same line, *furious about the cat*, and that single note is worth more to me than any careful description of the bird, and I have never been able to reproduce that in my own writing.

The second rule is that the notebook is a record of a time and a place, because a bird in a sentence with no date and no location is not a fact, it is the shape of a fact. And the third rule, which I **break** more than I keep, is that the notebook should contain at least one thing every week that the person writing it got wrong. An **excellent** year of notes still has three errors in it, and hunting them down is half the pleasure.

The most useful thing in it is a definition section she built without knowing she was building it, and I have started to steal from it. *Rain: not falling, arriving.* *Stream: a river that has not decided yet.* *Frost: the same water, less useful.* *Fog: cloud that has come down to have a look at you.* It is a **composition** of impression more than a scientific **catalog**, and it is much better than either.

The thing I have taken from her most seriously is the discipline of the ordinary. Most of the notebook is not about rare birds. It is about the regular ones — the ones that come every year, the ones you can predict, the ones that make a difference to a day. The remarkable thing is not that a rare bird turned up. The remarkable thing is that the same bird turned up for eleven years in a row, and that in year twelve it did not, and she noticed.

Because noticing is the whole discipline. Noticing is what you do before you have a word. And once you have a word, noticing gets easier, which is the problem, because the word is a kind of **filter** and the filter has a bias and the bias runs toward the things that already have names. I have caught myself seeing a dull bird as a dull bird and never once as a bird at all, and it took me years to understand how much of what I call the world is just the residue of my own vocabulary.

The one **weakness** in my grandmother's method, which I did not see until about ten years of doing it myself, is that she never once **combined** anything. She recorded, and recording is not thinking. She did not compare one April to another September, she did not **associate** a dry spring with anything she had read, and so the pattern that would have **emerged** from thirty years of her own notes simply sat there unformed, because nobody did the joining. Some days I think those notebooks are a perfect record of a mind that knew exactly how to look and had no idea at all how to think, and I cannot decide whether that is the **charm** of them or the whole limitation of them. Both, I think. Almost always both.

So I have started doing the thing she did not do. I take a season and I **incorporate** the weather into whatever else I was doing, so that the year has a shape and not just a list. It has made me slower, and it has made me **receive** a dry August differently. **Thus** the month no longer arrives as a complaint. It arrives as an event with a cause, a probable effect on the hedgerow, a near certainty about the reservoir, and an uneasy feeling about the field I have not sown. I do not **pray** about it, exactly, but I do talk to the year, and I associate it now with things I read and things I was told, and a great deal more emerges from it than it used to.

My brother finds this unendurable. He is a **democratic** man in most of the ways that word is used, and he cannot stand a person who appears to **kiss** a hedge, and we have had this argument for eleven years. He says I talk to the weather. I say the weather talks first. He says that is a charm argument and not an argument, and he is not wrong, and I have **awarded** him the point, and he was **delighted**, in the specific way of a man who has been let off.

I have never been a **prisoner** of this valley, and I have never been able to **afford** to live **anywhere** else, and the two facts turn out to be the same fact. You do not choose a place like this. You find out, one hedge at a time, that you have been living in it for eleven years.

Also he is wrong about the hedge.

This is the **narrative** problem and it is not a small one. Our whole account of nature is a **fiction** that we have agreed to call history, and the agreement is what lets us act, and the fiction is not a lie. A **novel** about a river is not trying to tell you what a river is. It is trying to tell you what a river is for, in the only way a story can, and those are not the same question.

## Digging the River Back

In 2019 a group of us spent a summer digging. The river had been straightened in the 1960s, cut into a long shallow line to move water to a farm faster, and it had done exactly that and also done several other things nobody wanted. The water table dropped. The **density** of gravel in the bed went up until the river was warm in July. Bank erosion took the leg of a footbridge and then the footbridge. An **original** bank plant, a small fern that had been there since anyone recorded it, was effectively gone within a decade.

So we took out a **pound** of gravel, by the tonne, and we put the river back roughly where the 1840 map said it had been, which is a sentence with an important hidden clause in it: *roughly*. The whole project cost less than one house. It is the best money our village council has ever spent, and I have watched two people who voted against it change their minds within about a year, which in this village counts as a moral conversion.

You learn a strange amount from a week of digging. The **hole** is never straight. Every time you clear one section a **wound** opens somewhere two meters away, where the water had quietly been weakening a bank nobody was watching. And you start to understand that the river's problem and the farmer's problem were never two problems. They were one problem wearing two coats.

The original complaint, though, was about a **stain** on the water. That sounds trivial and it is the opposite of trivial. A discoloration in a river is the most efficient **alarm** system in the world: it arrives before the fish do, and everybody who has ever fished or farmed near moving water knows it. We found the cause in a week. It was a **substance** nobody was supposed to be putting there, and the company that put it there was four hundred kilometers away and had no reason to think of us at all. **Hence** the letter-writing, and the meeting, and the slow fact of a corporation that treats a village as a hole in a map rather than a place with people in it.

What the project did to the summer was immediate and strange. Within a year the **rapid** shallow water had become slow deep water. Within two the temperature dropped, and a variety of fish that had not been seen in living memory came back up from somewhere downstream, and a man who has fished here since 1978 stood in the shallows and went quiet in a way I will not describe because it was private. The river does not know this happened. The river does not know anything. That is the most comforting thing about it and also, **occasionally**, the most **weird**.

We wrote it all up at the end, because a river group that does not write things up is a river group that has to start again in ten years. The report is long and mostly dull, and the **summary** at the front is four lines. My whole argument is that the four lines are the substance and the other ninety pages are how you earn the right to be believed by a council that has a budget meeting every third Tuesday in a room with a broken heater.

The meeting where this was argued is worth describing. The council is administrative in the way these bodies are: slow, careful, staffed by two people who between them know more about this valley than the entire committee. The **chair** is a farmer of sixty-one who has chaired the meeting for nine years and has learned the trick of listening to the whole room before he says anything, which is a skill I have never **possessed** and never will.

What I take from those meetings is mostly about how damaging it is to speak too soon. I have twice stood up in that room with a summary of the position and watched a **retired** engineer in the second row dismantle it in ninety seconds, gently, without any **offense** at all, by asking a question I had not thought to ask. Both times he was correct, and both times the position got stronger afterwards, because it had been corrected before it went public rather than after.

What I take from those meetings is also that nobody is the villain. The people who are genuinely **wealthy** and want the field flooded for a housing development are not monsters. They **desire** what they desire. My own position is a substance of vanity, habit, and a real attachment to a heron, and I do not get to be purer than that.

The one thing I would recommend is this: if you have to make an argument about a place, **dig** in it first, physically, for a day. There is no substitute. You will come back with better sentences, because a sentence that has not touched the actual mud is just a costume in borrowed clothes. People in that room can smell the difference, and it is not subtle, and it is not personal either.

### **Encounters**, Roughly Once a Month

Here is the honest part, and it is the part I did not know I was writing until I noticed how often these things had happened and roughly how often, which is about monthly, hence the frequency I have given, which I have counted by hand. The details **vary** every time, obviously. That is the point.

A **consistent** finding, across all of them: nothing happens. Things are there, and then they are not, and the **sum** of what I have learned in thirty years of looking out of windows is not a set of answers. It is a better **access** to the question, which is a **damn** sight more than I had at nineteen, and it cost me an effort I could not have spent any other way.

My one real **click**, if I am honest, was learning to do this at all from a man who found it **comedy** and nothing more. He used to hold the newspaper up to the light and make the funniest noises of distress about the weather, and I assume he was mocking me, and he was, and then one afternoon he **evolved** into just holding the paper up, and neither of us said a word about it, and that silence is the most **helpful** thing anyone has ever been able to **lend** me. You do not need a teacher. You need a person who does the thing badly in front of you until you try it.

**Firstly**: a **wave** of heat in July, the first one in my life, and I sat on the step at midnight in an absolutely stupid amount of heat and drank water and watched a **bell** sound three streets away with the windows open. Nothing dramatic. Just the specific fact of a body in a hot night, breathing.

Secondly: standing on a cliff, and the wind was strong enough that I felt the ground in my face, and the sea below had that rapid nervous quality of something huge that is also irritable and that I could not **protect** myself from or even out-wrestle. I felt very small and very much a person, which is a strange way to feel good.

Thirdly: a **slight** business of a fox in a garden, and I got close enough to see it **jump** a fence that I could not have jumped. And the fox saw me, and assessed, and I saw the assessment happen, and the fox decided I was not worth the trouble, and I was given the considerable gift of being politely ignored by something with better eyes than mine. I have thought about that encounter more than any other. It is also the only time I have ever been **defeated** so cleanly by anything, and I am still **impressed** by how completely it did it, and the two feelings are not as separate as I would like.

Fourthly: my grandmother's hand. She was eighty. The skin was very thin and the joints had that certain looseness, and I noticed that her wrist was no wider than my thumb, and the thing that got me was the lack of any surprise in her about it, the way she simply took her hand as being that hand, the whole adjustment already made in a place I could not see. I cried, which she ignored, in the way that people ignore things they have decided to.

Fifthly: a night of rain so heavy that the sound changed character halfway through, from a noise to a pressure, and I had the impulse to get up and stand in it, and I did not, and I have never been sure whether the not-going was good judgment or cowardice, and I have mostly decided it was good judgment, and I still think about it.

Occasionally, in a gap in all of that, there is nothing. The field is a field. The wall is a substance of stone. The road goes where roads go, and the birds do what birds do, and there is no lesson, and no meaning, and no message I am supposed to take from it. Days like this I now think are the original purpose, and everything else is a layer we painted on.

And I have a summary habit, the one genuinely useful thing I have taken from all of this. When I cannot describe what I saw, I record only the weather and the date. No interpretation, no story, no moral. Just *overcast, 4 degrees, the light went at ten past three.* If I did that for ten years I would have a record of what this place looked like at the moments when I was not performing my own life, and I would not have to dig any of it back up, and the writing would be reliable because it never had to carry a weight that writing is not supposed to carry.

## The Part That Is Hard, and the Part That Is Not

I want to be honest about the difficulty here, because the rest of this is too easy.

My grandmother's notebook is a record of a world where a woman in a village in 1962 could keep a barometer on her wall and her garden in order and never once have to read a newspaper to know what kind of year it would be. That world is not gone. But it is no longer the default, and the part of it that most people miss is not the technology. The part they miss is the shift in what counts as information. A forecast used to be a conversation with a neighbor who was wrong sometimes. It is now apparently a mental health matter, which is a strange and sad line to have crossed.

I do not think we get the world back by wanting it back. But there is a real difference between a person who knows the name of a plant and a person who has spent an afternoon failing to find out what it is called, and the difference is not knowledge. It is the experience of not knowing and staying in the room anyway. I have had maybe four of those afternoons. I remember all four, and I remember what the light was doing, and I did not write any of it down.

The other hard thing is that I do not have a conclusion. I can tell you the river bends there, and the bird comes in April, and the field goes through its yellow phase in the second week of July. I cannot tell you what any of it is for. And I have noticed that the absence of a conclusion is much easier to sit with when you have at least one real skill, one thing your hands can do that another person's hands cannot, and the connection between the two of them.

### A Small List of Things I Would Recommend

My grandmother left no **recommendation**. She left a barometer, a notebook, and a set of habits, and the habits did not come with instructions. I have since made my own list, and it is short, and half of it is **embrace** of things I used to find embarrassing.

Do not buy a **asset** you cannot look after. This applies to land, to dogs, to instruments, and to most of the strong feelings people have about their own future. The exceptions are bicycles and **ski** equipment, because those you will use until they break, which is the correct way to own anything.

When a **discussion** gets loud, write the summary of both sides before you reply. I have never once regretted this and I have regretted almost everything else.

If you **purchase** anything for the outdoors, buy the **adequate** version. I own one excellent rope I do not need and four adequate ropes I use weekly, and I will not be examining the excellent one again.

Go outside anywhere at least weekly, and go with **multiple** purposes or none. The single-purpose walk, the one where you are trying to fix yourself, is the one that stops happening by February.

Go **personally** and go anywhere strange once a year. Go somewhere the local recommendation would not send you. The point is not the place. The point is the **dare**.

Do not ignore the first time something feels wrong in your body, and do not dare to wait for the second.

Be adequate, not excellent. This is a **conservative** position and I have made my peace with it.

**Bind** loose pages. This is absurd advice and I am giving it anyway, because I lost nine pages of my grandmother's notebook in 1998 and I still do not know what was on them, and a multiple of that is worse, and I have learned this the expensive way.

## What the Barometer Knows

The barometer is in the hallway and it is not a good barometer. It is old and it is subjective in the way all old instruments are, and it should be checked every year against something modern, and I have not done it, and I do not plan to.

But it is accurate enough for what I ask of it, which is: not a number, but a status. Is the day settling, or is it about to turn. The needle does not know what rain is. It knows that the air is heavier or lighter, and every time it moves, something is about to happen, and the something is always something I did not plan. It **reminds** me, in the way that old instruments do, that my own senses are useful but stale, and I have no way to check them, and I have never once assumed they were right when the barometer said otherwise.

I have stopped pretending this is a metaphor. The barometer is a tool and it is honest and it is limited, and those three facts together are the best description I have of any relationship in my life, and I have had several.

The window faces north and the river is four miles away and the field will be yellow in July. I write all of this down because I promised my grandmother I would, and because a record is a kind of respect, and because in about forty years somebody will find this notebook and be curious about it, and the least they deserve is an accurate description of what a person in this valley, in this decade, actually saw out the window.

If you want the practical version of all this, [A Day in the Life](01-%E6%97%A5%E5%B8%B8%E7%94%9F%E6%B4%BB%20A%20Day%20in%20the%20Life.md) covers what an ordinary working day actually asks of you, and [How Work Actually Works](02-%E5%B7%A5%E4%BD%9C%20How%20Work%20Actually%20Works.md) has the other side of it, the part where the outside world does not schedule itself. Between the two of them, that is most of what I know.

## What the Window Is For

I have said nothing about why. It is not for the weather, and it is not for the river, and it is not even for the notebook. Those are the **mere** mechanics. The window is in the house because a person needs one direction to look in that is nobody else's business — not a screen, not a street, not a face, not a field of somebody else's hopes.

My grandmother kept her window facing the lane for sixty-one years and never once described the view in a poem, or in a letter, or to me. She just looked. And in about forty years somebody will find her notebook and be curious about what a person in that valley, in that decade, actually saw out of the window, and the least she deserved is that it was true.

I think that is the whole of it. The barometer, the notebook, the river, the yellow field in July. Not a number, but a direction.
