# How Work Actually Works

这一篇讲工作的真实运作：工厂、办公室、面试、薪水与绩效，以及一份工作到底给了你什么。

## Hello, and What Work Is For

Hello. Before we begin, I want to say one thing plainly: work is not the thing you imagine at twenty-two. At twenty-two I thought work was a **career** — a clean line that goes up. Work is not a line. It is a set of **procedures**, repeated, in bad weather, with other people who are also tired.

My first real job was at a small **factory** in a **county** town in western China. I was twenty-three. I had a **degree** in a subject that nobody outside my family could name, and I had no idea what a **day** in a factory was like. Almost the first thing I learned was how to stand still for eight hours. My feet hurt. My **knee** hurt. In the third week I understood that most of what people call "working hard" is really just staying **regular** and quiet for a long time.

The factory made small metal parts for water pipes. A **manufacturer** in another city sent us the drawings. My **task** was to check each part against a paper **document**, and write down the number of parts that were bad. I wrote the number on a line in a big book. That was the whole job. It was **basically** data collection, and I did it for eleven months.

What I did not understand then was that this book was the most powerful object in the building. The **manager** read it every morning. If the number of bad parts went up, we stopped. If it went down, he wrote down **why** it went down. That is the whole **strategy** of almost every factory in the world: measure one number, every day, without fail, and act on it.

I want to be clear about something. **Learning** a job is not the same as learning a subject. A subject gives you knowledge. A job gives you a **skill** — a way of doing one narrow thing well enough that somebody will pay you. The knowledge is nice. The skill pays the **wage**.


A factory has a very simple **logic**, even when it looks complicated from outside. **Production** begins with a **customer** who wants something. Somebody in an office must **advertise** the product. Somebody else must **apply** for a **license** and a **permit** from the government, and must obey every **regulation** and every **law**. A **branch** office must sign a **contract** with a **supplier** who sells the steel. Then a **crew** on the floor makes the thing, a **section** of workers tests it, and a **dealer** somewhere far away sells it. If any one of these steps **fail**, the whole chain is late.

When the chain works well, the factory is a beautiful machine. When it does not, the price of the product goes up, and the **consumer** pays more at the shop. This is not an opinion. It is **data**. Our **manager** showed us a **percent** graph of the cost of one bad part: it was tiny, one percent, and it made the whole order lose money. That graph changed how the line behaved more than any speech from the top.

The word that stuck with me was **performance**. Not English grammar performance. The performance of a machine, of a **team**, of a person. A factory is a machine for improving performance. You **reduce** waste, you cut cost, you raise the **rate** of good parts, and the **standard** rises a little each month. Nobody gives a speech about this. It just happens, quietly, month after month, or it does not happen at all.

There is a **strong** temptation in this work to think only about **money**. But the strongest thing I took from the factory floor was **knowledge** and a **vision** of how a real **industry** operates. I could look at a product and ask: who **funds** this, who regulates it, who buys it, who profits from it, and where does the **revenue** go. That question has been useful in every job since.

## The Office, and What Nobody Writes Down

My second job was in an **office**, and it was a completely different animal. I worked for a small **firm** that sold services to other companies. There were about forty people on the top floor. We had a **department** for each kind of work, and a **policy** about everything — how many days of holiday we could take, how long we could speak on the **telephone**, how many pages a report could be.

Rules like these have a bad name. People say the office is full of red tape. That is not quite fair. Most of these **rules** exist because somebody, once, made a serious mistake. The rule is a scar from an old pain. But nobody keeps the story, so the rule looks like nonsense.

I once waited three weeks for a signature. A single **document** had to move from one **division** to another, and no one had the authority to move it. A whole **project** sat behind that one page of paper. I was twenty-six and I wanted to shout. Now I know: the person who could sign was on a **business trip** three thousand kilometers away, and he was, himself, waiting for another document. This is what **leadership** actually costs.

What nobody writes down is **behavior**. Work is 20 percent the job you were hired to do, and 80 percent how you behave while doing it. Do you **organize** your files, or do you leave a mess for the next person? Do you **share** what you know, or do you keep it so that you look important? Do you tell a colleague the truth about a **mistake**, or do you hide it until it becomes an **error** that costs the company money? A small error is a lesson. The same error in month six is a **pattern**, and a pattern is a problem.

There is also a quiet competition, and nobody tells you about it. Everyone in the office is **busy**. The list of urgent things is always longer than the day. The people who **rise** are not always the cleverest. They are the ones who are **keen** on the difficult project, not the easy one. They say yes when the task is dull. They finish the small thing nobody asked for, and they **complete** it the same day.

## An Interview, and the Job You Did Not Get

In my third year I began to **apply** for other work, and I learned what an **interview** really is. It is not a test of knowledge. It is a **negotiation**, and it is also a performance. Two people are sitting there, both afraid of making a mistake. I am afraid I am wasting their time. They are afraid they are hiring the wrong **person** for two years.

I once interviewed with a **chairman** of a manufacturing firm. He was a quiet man in his sixties. He asked me about my best result, and I gave him a fine answer. Then he said: "Now tell me about a time everything went wrong." I said something clever and empty. He did not say no, but his **expression** changed, and I knew the answer before I left the room.

I **criticize** myself for that answer still. A better answer would have been: here is a month that fell apart, here is what I did on the third day, here is what it cost, here is what I learned. Failure is an **exhibition** of your own quality, if you know how to display it. Almost nobody does.

I also learned that **qualification** matters less than people say, though not as little as other people say. A **university** **degree** opens the first door. A **professional** certificate opens the second. But a **specialist** who is known in one narrow field can be hired without either. And a good **consultant** or **adviser** is hired for judgment, which no **exam** can test.

Three other things I wish someone had told me at twenty-three. First, **prepare** the story about your worst day, not your best day. Second, ask one good question about their **objective** for the year — the interviewer's face will tell you whether they are really looking for anything. Third, when they offer you a **salary**, do not accept in the room. Say that you are very **grateful** and will come back tomorrow. Almost nobody does this, and the ones who do usually get a better number.

## Money: Salary, Wage, and the Number Nobody Shows You

Money at work is almost never simple, and most people learn it far too late. Let me try to say it in one paragraph, and I apologize in advance.

A company sells something. The money coming in is **income**, or **revenue**. The money going out is **expenditure**: **wages** for the **employees**, rent, machines, electricity, the **brand** work, the office **expenses**, and the **fee** you pay your accountant. What is left is **profit**. When the outgoings are larger than the income, the company has a **deficit**, and then everyone becomes very careful, very fast, and often very unhappy.

Some of this money is not yours at all. Your **employer** must take a **tax** from your pay before you see it. Your **salary** may be split into a base pay and a **variable** part, so that a bad month is a small month. And a part of what you earn may go into a **pension**, which is money that you will not touch for thirty years. When people say "invest in your career," they usually mean a **course**, not a bank account, but the same word serves both.

A **campaign**, a **campaign** manager, a **brand** — these words are about how a company spends to make you want what it sells. A strong brand lets a company pay a **lower** cost. A weak brand makes every **dealer** push harder. An **investor** looks at all of this and decides whether to put money in. If the **budget** does not rise, the **expense** has to fall, and somebody's project dies. That is the real chain, and it is not a moral story, but it has a moral shape: most of what happens at work happens to somebody.


Here is the part of work that nobody puts in the job **description**. Everything **falls** apart. A project falls behind. A customer leaves. A machine breaks at six in the morning. A good worker walks out of the door and the whole team has to **struggle** for a quarter. The **rate** of these things seems to rise every year, and I do not think that is only in my head.

The **pressure** is a real problem, not a sign of weakness. I have seen good people harm themselves with stress — not **harm** other people, harm their own health — because they never learned to say no. They say yes to every project, they stay two hours past closing, and then in the third year their body simply stops. A colleague of mine needed **surgery** and was **unable** to work for half a year. He had no **insurance** and the cost **ruined** his family. There is a simple rule here that took me too long to learn: work is not the place where you prove what you can survive. It is the place where you find out what you are good at.

On a bad day the office is very **loud** and everyone is very **tired**. What helps is not a big speech. It is to **define** the problem in one clear **statement**, decide the next small step, and do it today. Big ideas do not help. A short list of what will happen this week helps. An **indication** that you are moving helps. Silence helps less than you think.

## The Job You Actually Have

If you ask me what work actually is, I will not give you a definition. I will give you a picture.

It is a school day that starts at seven. It is a **lecture** hall with ninety students and a **professor** who is ten minutes late. It is the **principal** of a school asking you for a **grade** you did not know you had to hand in. It is a **appointment** you made three weeks ago, and the person you came to see is **unable** to see you, and you are still **unable** to leave. It is a **talent** you did not know you had until somebody gave you a hard project. It is a **native** speaker correcting your accent with a **gentle** smile. It is a **stranger** on the phone who is angry, and then is not angry, and then thanks you.

It is also the part you never see. The **document** you read in ten minutes took three people three days. The **expert** advice came from a person who spent ten years on it, paid for it, and still was **unable** to explain it in one sentence. The **vision** of a company was written by one **executive** on a train in 2011, and now it appears on a slide in every meeting. The **officer** who signs the **clause** at the bottom of the contract may not understand it, but the company cannot **withdraw** it. That is how work works. Everything simple was once extremely hard for somebody.

Some smaller images, because work is mostly small things. A **pile** of paper on a desk that nobody will clear. A **pipe** that bursts in a **location** nobody wants to visit. A boy playing **football** in the empty space behind the factory on a Sunday. My mother's **hair** turned white in three years, between one job and the next. A **rat** that lived under the office floor and stole a sandwich. A **seal** on a letter that is worth more than everything inside it. A **breath** you take before you answer a question that will decide your next year.



Once a week the whole **staff** met on a Monday morning. A **director** who I barely knew stood at the front of the room and read numbers out loud. Every department had a **goal** for the month, and every department had to say whether it had reached it. The word for that word is **operation**: the daily work of a company, as opposed to its plans.

I hated those meetings when I was twenty-five. They felt **totally** useless, a **huge** waste of an hour. Now I think they were the most valuable hour of my week. Not because of the numbers, but because they made the numbers **visible**. A team can hide failure for a long time, quietly, as long as nobody puts it on a screen. One Monday morning it is impossible to hide.

You learn a great deal from watching how a **corporate** room behaves when something has gone badly. Watch the **appearance**. Some people sit very still. Some people start an **interaction** with the person next to them, quietly, under the noise — that is where the real information is. Watch the **primary** speaker. Does she **acknowledge** the bad news, or does she step over it? If she steps over it, you may **hesitate** to bring her anything difficult next week, and the whole company loses a month of information. This is not a soft subject. It is where careers are actually made or lost.

I remember one meeting where the number was terrible. The director was quiet for a while, and then she said, "I **propose** we look at this **briefly**, not at the person." That sentence saved a good worker from being pushed out of the door, and it saved me from learning the wrong lesson. There is a big difference between a **failure** and a **mistake**, and a company that cannot tell them apart will **destroy** itself inside three years, no matter how much money it has.



Nobody tells you the **schedule** of a career, and the schedule is not what you expect. It is not a ladder with steps at even distances. Sometimes you wait two years and then move very fast; sometimes you move sideways and it counts for more.

What I have seen work, again and again, is this. You need one thing that is **strong** and real, and you need the **confidence** to say no. Confidence is not pride. Confidence is **knowledge** — it is the quiet feeling that you know what you are doing, and that when somebody pushes back, you can **explain** your reasons. A confident person can be **polite** and still hold a line.

You also need a good **boss**. This sounds obvious. It is not. A boss is not a person who knows the most. A boss is a person who tells you the **duty** clearly, gives you room to fail, and does not take credit in front of other people. Find one. If your **duty** is always changing and nobody will write it down, ask for a short description of the job. When I did this, my employer **accomplished** three months of confusion in one afternoon, simply by writing six lines.

Promotion, when it comes, is almost always **beyond** the job description. It comes from the second thing you do well, not the first. The first thing you do well, they paid you for. So look for the second thing: the project nobody has claimed, the problem everybody calls **crazy** until somebody fixes it, the customer nobody wants to call at night.

And be careful with your **intention**. Almost everybody **intend** to be good workers. Good intention is cheap and it does not **achieve** anything by itself. What **achieves** something is a **schedule**: three hours a week, on Tuesday, for a year. Most people give up in March. Most companies are **broad** enough that a person who quietly becomes **strong** in one corner will be found, always, and usually sooner than they expect.



Work is mostly conversation, and most of us are bad at it because nobody trains us. We **translate** our thoughts into sentences in a hurry, and then wonder why the other person **hesitated**.

Some words that came up again and again in my office, and what they really meant. "**Can you take a look at this?**" almost never means only that. It means: I am behind, and I am aware that I am asking too much. A good answer is: "Yes. By when do you need it?" — the two questions **respectively** are about capacity and about time, and the second one is the one you were asked.

"**No problem.**" means the problem is now yours. "**Let me take a look.**" means genuinely "no problem." "**I will try.**" means almost no. "**I will do it.**" means yes. These four sentences are worth more in an office than any computer skill.

If you **create** a **brief** of an hour at the start of a project, you will save a week. Say what the problem is, what the **primary** goal is, what must **happen** this week, what is not important, and who decides. That kind of **communication** is the most **efficient** tool I know and it costs nothing.

There is also a real difference between a **specific** question and a broad one. "Is the report ready?" is a broad question. It gets you nothing. "Is chapter three finished, and do you need the tables from me today?" gets you an answer and a plan. Broad questions **create** **complexity**. Specific ones **reduce** it.

And here is the strange part. Almost every conflict I have seen at work came from a **primary** subject that was **tiny** — a word in a document, a tone in a **speech**, a number that was read wrongly — and grew large because nobody said anything. When you see the first small bad **species** of it, say it early. Say it **directly**. Say it kindly, but say it.



Work is expensive. This is not a complaint; it is a fact, and you should know it before you are thirty.

An hour costs something. A weekend costs something. A **farm** holiday, an old friend's wedding in another **province** — these cost more than money, and no **corporate** budget can pay them back. If you do not know the price, you will spend it without thinking, and then one day you will look up and find that you have no **monthly** routine, no friend who knows your **major** worries, and no **season** in the year that belongs to you alone.

Work also takes health. Mine, first, and then the mental kind. **Strain** is not proof of devotion. A **acute** pain in the neck that lasts a month is not a personality. I know two people who left **satisfying** and well-paid work because their bodies could not take it any more, and I think they were brave, and I have never entirely forgiven myself for admiring them from a comfortable chair.

What does work buy? Not only money. It buys a **major** sense of your own **capability**: knowing, in a deep quiet way, that you can take a thing you know nothing about and get it done. That **confidence** is the real product of a career. It does not **restrain** you; it lets you **enormous** things. It also buys you a strange **happiness**, the kind that is **pure** and short. A thing you made works. A **primary** colleague thanks you. The team goes home. That is it. I have never found that happiness anywhere else with the same **intensity**, and I have looked.

And sometimes work buys you **harm**, too. If you are in a place where you cannot **breathe**, where the **opinion** of one person decides the whole atmosphere, where you must lie to survive — get out, **immediately**. A bad year at the right company is a lesson. A bad decade at the wrong one is a **severe** cost. There is no **excuse** that repairs it, and every person I know who stayed too long has said some version of the same thing afterward: "I should have gone **earlier**."



There is a **process** inside every career, and it is much slower than people expect. You do one thing for two years, then another thing for two years, and somewhere around year six you look up and notice that the **application** of your skill has become a **profession** — that is, a body of work with a name, with an **effective** set of rules, with people who can **solve** problems you could not solve when you started.

Most people **pass** through this stage without noticing. They **join** a company, they **enjoy** the work for a while, and then they ask a hard question: is this where I stop? The honest answer is that a company is a good place to **learn** and a bad place to hide. If the work no longer teaches you anything, staying is a **model** of a decision you are making with your life.

I **would** put it this way. Work is the largest **contest** you will ever **join**, and the **champion** is not the strongest person in the room but the one who is still standing and still improving in year ten. Nobody **assigns** that to you. You have to do it yourself, and almost nobody does, which is why almost nobody is happy at fifty.



Here are some small, **practical** things that took me years to understand.

**Purpose**. If you cannot answer "what is this work for?" in one sentence, you will burn out. A **mission** that you can say out loud in front of another person is worth more than a salary increase.

**Agreement**. Get everything in writing, even in a friendly office: the **terms**, the hours, the **payment**, the notice period. An oral **agreement** that both parties remember differently is a **dispute** waiting for a bad month. This is not distrust; it is **honor** on both sides.

**Qualification**. If a course, a **certificate**, or a **license** is required for the work, get it early. Do not wait until the **demands** arrive.

**Former** colleagues are the best **source** of the next job you will get. Keep their numbers. **Greet** them once a year with no **purpose** other than the greeting. This habit will **honor** you.

**Duty**. Know the difference between what you must do, what you agreed to do, and what you **ought** to do. The third one is what makes a career a career.

**Loss**. Ask what happens when this project **fails**. Who pays, who is **unable** to accept it, what is the **record** you will have to write. Be **ready**.



A **driver** at the gate who raised the barrier every morning for six years and never once complained, and who knew the name of every person in the building. A **university** professor who took an hour after every lecture to answer one student's question, and **illustrated** it on the board. An old **chief** who said: "I do not want your opinion. I want your **suggestion**, and then I will decide." A **commissioner** who could not spell, and who nonetheless never made a bad decision about a person. A **foreigner** who worked here for three years and still, every **autumn**, sent a card with no **remark** on it at all.

And the fact that a single **dollar** in a worker's pocket and the same dollar in a manager's pocket behave completely differently — that the same **sum** means **average** comfort in one place and quiet **terror** in another. That gap is not in the money. It is in what the money **ought** to buy.

## Small Moments I Would Keep

If you strip work down to what actually stays with you, it is very short.

My first **teacher** at the factory was an old **worker** who had never left the **county**. He could hold a **pipe** in one hand and a screwdriver in the other and still hear what you said across the floor. He never became a manager and never wanted to. When he died, forty people came, and the quality of the eulogy was not about skill. It was about **happiness**, which he had clearly been giving away for free his whole life.

There was a young **worker** in my office who could read a complicated contract faster than any lawyer I have met. She became senior in five years. She once told me: "I don't want to be liked. I want to be **correct**, and I want to be kind. Those three things do not **conflict** as often as people think."

A **brand** manager once flew four hours to visit a factory that made a small part, and stood in the dust for two hours asking questions. The workers did not understand why. Afterwards I understood: she was chasing the whole **insight** of where her product actually came from, because without it every **sentence** she wrote was **empty**.

An old colleague used to **quote** a **native** saying to every new employee: "First, come **often**. Second, come early. Third, do not come at all." It seemed **crazy** for a month, and then I understood it, and it has been true for thirty years.

And a stranger at a conference told me the most useful thing about **advance** planning: "**Complex** things are not hard because they are large. They are hard because they are not **clear**. Make them clear and they are easy." I have **applied** this advice more times than I can count. I **evaluate** every big project I have ever run by one question only — what is the next step, and who does it — and the answer has never once been the wrong thing to ask.


There is a **process** inside every career, and it is much slower than people expect. You do one thing for two years, then another thing for two years, and somewhere around year six you look up and notice that the **application** of your skill has become a **profession** — a body of work with a name, with an **effective** set of rules, with people who can **solve** problems you could not solve when you started.

Most people **pass** through this stage without noticing. They **join** a company, they **enjoy** the work for a while, and then they ask a hard question: is this where I stop? **Although** the answer is no, it is easy to stay for years. The honest answer is that a company is a good place to **learn** and a bad place to hide. If the work no longer teaches you anything, staying is a **model** of a decision you are making with your life.

I would put it this way. Work is the largest **contest** you will ever **join**, and the **champion** is not the strongest person in the room but the one still standing and still improving in year ten. Nobody **assigns** that to you. You have to do it yourself, and almost nobody does, which is why almost nobody is happy at fifty.

One more thing, **independent** of all this: if you ever feel that your work no longer **examines** your values — if the only question left is how to keep a job that has stopped teaching you — **leave**. **Fill** the **arm** of your hand with something else. People who **reject** a **polish** salary to do work they **respect** are not romantic; they are simply **ready** for what comes next. **Locate** the courage **before** you need it, because the moment you need it you will not have time to find it. Do not let a job **thicken** you into somebody you would rather not **sit** beside.

**Success**, if it comes, comes the same way as everything else here: slowly, quietly, and **significantly** less often than you hope.


My old department had a **user** manual that nobody had read since it was printed. When the new system came in, the **investment** in training was almost zero, and the **shall** of every plan went out the window. Two of us spent a **sequence** of weeks rewriting it. Our manager, who had been **proud** of that system once, called it **sufficient** and never said thank you.

The company I work for now is **contemporary** in the way these things usually are: half modern, half asleep. The new building has an **accommodation** for people who work late, and a rule about the **tail** of the day. My desk is **beneath** a window. On the other side of the street a man tunes old clocks, and the sound is **random** and regular at the same time. I have come to **anticipate** that sound the way sailors anticipate weather.

Work also gives you people you would **greatly** underestimate. A **virtual** team of nine, spread across six cities, did more in one year than our office of sixty did in five. They were **secondary** to everybody's mind until the numbers arrived, and then they were not. I **restore** that memory whenever I am tempted to judge people by where they sit.

Whenever I teach a new hire, I give the same **guideline**: strong **structural** habits matter more than **theoretical** knowledge, and both matter far less than kindness. That is the whole method, and it is **intense** enough.


A few more things I remember. A **bit** of luck is worth more than a **bit** of talent in most offices, and I have never been able to argue the opposite. A factory in another city **traded** with ours for years before both of us closed, and nobody writes about that kind of history. When a supplier fails, somebody must **drop** the order in a **partly** angry state and start the **silver**-tongued work of finding another one.

A good manager can **threaten** without meaning harm, which is a rare skill. I saw one woman end a bad meeting by saying, quite calmly, "If we do not **rescue** this project today, I will have to explain the loss in **partly** a very short way." Everyone worked until nine. Nobody enjoyed it. It **strengthened** the team more than a year of kind speeches.

Do not be **virtual** about your career. In 2021 our whole team worked **virtually** for eight months, and it taught me that the office was never the work itself. A **restricted** life, a job that forbids you one honest opinion, a team that punishes a question — **strengthen** yourself by being somewhere else as soon as you can. You may **rescue** yourself years before anybody notices you needed it.

## How to Begin

If you are starting now, my advice is plain and short. **Focus** on the one **skill** that nobody can take from you, and get **stronger** at it than the person next to you. Keep your **professional** name clean — an ethical worker in a **legal** industry is not a cliche, it is the cheapest advantage in the market. Learn to write a clear, short **statement** of what you did in a way a stranger can understand. Be the person who is ready when the difficult project arrives, not the person who is free. **Track** your own progress: what you could do a year ago, what you can do now, and what you cannot yet do at all.

**Education** is the shortest road and not the only one. A **college** year is worth a lot; a bad job for four years is worth more, and not always in the way you expect. Read one good **book** a month **beside** your **sector**, and you will be more **valuable** than most people ten years ahead of you.

And do not look at the salary page too early. The **salary** will follow the work; it rarely comes first. Almost every strong **professional** I know has at some point been paid too little, and almost every unhappy one I know was only counting money.

Some lessons came from outside. In a **sector** I knew nothing about, I met a professor who had spent thirty years on one narrow question and was never **bored** for a single day. That is the strongest argument for **education** I have ever heard. A colleague once told me the best idea of his career came from a **conversation** with a **gardener**. A **publisher** in another city once paid me for a small piece of writing, and the **prize** was not money — it was the knowledge that somebody far away found my thinking **useful**.

Some days are **awful** and some are **favor**-able, and the **topic** never changes. A **drama** in the office, an **accident** on the line, a **bomb** of news at nine in the morning, a **poll** of numbers that came out **fat** with good news. You **restore** your calm, you **shut** the door, you **concentrate** on the next thing, and you go home. A **sharp** day and a flat day both end at the same hour. That is the whole **comfort**, and it is not a small comfort.

Some small **pleasure** to finish on. A **length** of paper you wrote that somebody read all the way to the end. A **length** of time you gave to a **stranger** who needed it. A hospital visit made on a **tuesday** afternoon that you did not want to make, and afterwards **grateful** for. And the knowledge that tomorrow, the work will still be there, and so will you, and it will still **tolerate** your presence for one more day.
