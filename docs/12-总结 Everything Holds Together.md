# Everything Holds Together

一个人换过城市、换过工作，最后真正留下来的不是地址和职位，而是他用来安放自己的那几句话。

## A Man with Several Names

I have had four **names**. None of them was my idea.

The first one belongs to a small town in the north, to parents who were young, and to a **year** that my mother still will not discuss. The second was given to me by a school office, spelled badly, and I used it for two years before I found out. The third I chose myself at twenty, in a **city** whose name I could not pronounce. The fourth was spelled differently for legal reasons and turned out to be the best one I have.

I am forty-four now. I have lived in six places, held four jobs, and buried one **man** who taught me almost everything I know about money. My **life** is a fairly ordinary thing, which is exactly why I am willing to set it down in words.

I have never had a plan. There was no plan. There was a series of **problems**, and each time one closed another **opened**, and I moved toward whatever light was nearest. That is a **kind** of living that nobody writes a book about.

What holds it together is not the four names or the four jobs. It is a habit of saying things to myself. Most of those sentences I did not learn from anybody. I made them, one at a time, the way a wall is made out of small stones, and they are the only **part** of me that has never had to move.

## The Small Machinery of a Morning

I get up early, earlier than almost anyone I know. It is not discipline. It is the **hour** — at six the **head** is clear and the **face** has not yet decided what expression to wear.

The first hour has a **small** set of steps, and the **order** of the steps is always the same. Water. Tea. The window. I do not **look** at a screen for the first thirty minutes, and I **keep** that rule even in the old years when the work was hard and I was not sure I could do it. It is not about calm. It is that a **man** who starts the day by reading other people's opinions has already given the day away, and there is **no** way to get it back.

After the tea I **read**, ten pages of anything, sometimes a **book** that has nothing to do with my work. I do not always understand it. That stopped mattering a long time ago. What I take from it is not information but a **kind** of company. On the bad days it is the news: a **murder** in the next town, a **weapon** left on a train, a **governor** promising a **presidential** thing he will never do. I read those too. A man has to know what his city is about, and most of what a city is about is **blood**, and money, and the **shoot** somebody is about to fire.

Then breakfast, and then the small **business** of being a body. I wash, I dress, I make the **room** look like somebody lives in it. This takes eleven minutes. I have counted. The **house** can wait until the weekend; the **home** cannot wait at all, because home is the thing you carry.

I am a slow starter and I do not fight this any more. I would rather begin **small** and do it **again** the next **day** than begin big and stop by Thursday.

I read more than I play, and play more than I used to. When I was young there was a **group** of us and a **court** and something happening every evening, and I **learned** then that a body which **moves** early in the day is a body that behaves later. I was a **child**, and the knowledge sat there **un**til somebody in an office pointed it out.

I think often about my father, who was a **man** of few words and who believed that a day should be judged at night, not in the morning. When he died the thing I missed was not the man. It was the order of the sentences.

## The Job, and the Job Before It

I have worked in a **company** that made metal parts, in a **company** that sold insurance in a market street, in a school, and now for a small business that no one outside it has heard of. Four jobs. Three **countries**. One woman, my sister, and one very long argument about money that we had in the year I turned thirty.

The first job was at night. I worked in a big open room where the machines were louder than the radio, and I learned to think inside noise. I do not recommend it, but I would not give it back. The men there played cards during the breaks, and I was the one who **must** buy the coffee for everybody, which taught me more **about** a group of people than any school did. I still **thank** the night shift for the only education I did not pay for.

The second job taught me how **people** talk when they want something. I sat at a desk and I **build** small files, and I learned that almost every hard conversation in that office began with a **case** number. Numbers are a kind of peace. When you have a number, you do not have to look at the **face** of the other person.

The third job was in a **school**, and I was there to fix machines, but I stayed two years longer than the job required, because a **student** can make you feel useful in a way no manager ever did.

The current job is the fourth. I read papers, I write a **report** every week, I sit in a **group** meeting where people use the **word** "alignment" in a way that still makes me tired, and I do the arithmetic. It pays **money** that is **less** than the first job and **more** than the second. It is enough, and I **spend** carefully.

There is a **program** for young people at the business next door, and I have **bought** their coffee more times than I should have, because a young person will tell you the **true** thing about a market if you **bring** them a cup and sit down. I do a **presentation** every **month** to **shareholders** who will not be in the room, and I keep a **disk** of the old numbers on a shelf, because a number is a **membership** card in a club you cannot resign from.

Money is the one subject on which I have no poetry. A **cost** that is small can still ruin you. An hour that is badly chosen can take a **year** out of you. My father used to say that a man who cannot **state** his costs in one sentence has not understood his own life. I was twenty-four then and I thought a **statement** was a thing that banks made.

In every job there was a moment when I could have **become** something else. I never took those moments, but the reason I stayed was often of one kind only, which is that I could not think of a second option. I **consider** it cowardice some days and good sense on others. A man who **leads** a team of five has to learn to say nothing for a long time, and that one skill covers most of the difficult **areas** of a small business. It is also the skill you use **against** your own worst habit, which is talking when you should be waiting.

## The Cities, and the Road Between Them

I have moved more than anybody in my family. I know the shape of a city better than I know the shape of a **week** — where the cheap food is, which bus goes where, which street lights up at night. When I land somewhere **new** I do not look for a job first. I walk the same road for three days and I learn the **market**, the school, and the place where the old men sit in the afternoon. I find out where things are **before** I find out what they cost.

The first city had a river in it. I lived there for two years and did almost nothing but study at night. Everything I know about how to learn, I learned there, in a **high** room above a laundry, at a table that moved when the truck went past. I was twenty and thought that discomfort was a **sign** of a real life. I still think so, but I also think it was a **sign** of a cheap **room**. A **course** of that kind is a **great** deal of education and I would not send a **child** through it.

The second city was the opposite. Too much **history**, too much **interest** in the past, and I was bored. I learned that a city can be beautiful and still be unkind to a person who is not interesting. **Though** it was a **good** school district and I **really** wanted the work, I **left** after eight months, and that was the first time I trusted a small feeling over a large plan.

The third was the one I learned to work in. Big, hard, too much traffic, and there I became a man who could be trusted with a **case**. It was the largest of the four and the one I knew best **after** the second year. I still go back once a year and I always feel **high** and slightly ill. There are many reasons for that, and not all of them are about the distance. The **architecture** there is **second** rate, there is a **tank** in the square where the buses turn, and no **historian** would bother to write about it. The last **championship** the football club won was before I was born, and a boy in the office still wears the shirt.

The fourth is where I live now. It is small. It is a **place** where people know each other in a way that took me eighteen months to learn, and where nobody **seems** to want me to be anybody. The houses here are **old** and the buildings **under** repair last for years, and nobody here thinks a broken road is a national event. It suits me. Everything I need is **around** the corner, and everything I do not need is a long way **away**.

Between all of them there is a road, and the road is where a lot of my thinking gets done. I do not like trains, which is a strange thing for a man with a case file about cross-border freight, but I do not care. I like being on it for hours because the world is going past and I am going too, and there is no **point** at which I could be asked to decide anything.

If you want to know why I keep travelling, it is not a search for meaning. It is that I have found something about myself which I can only see from a distance: I am a man who is not finished yet, and the day I stop moving he will still be there, unfinished, waiting. I do not think I will **leave** this last city. But I like to know that I **could**.

## How I Know What I Know

I am not a scientist. I worked in a lab for one summer and I was not good at it. But something I learned there has stayed with me, and it has to do with **information**.

The first lesson is that a **fact** is a small thing with a very particular shape. A fact can be checked. A number can be checked. An opinion, however well written, cannot be checked, and a person who is **certain** of an opinion is not more right than one who is **sure** of a fact.

The second lesson is about the **source**. A number that comes from a **group** of people who were paid to count is a different kind of number from a number that comes from a **group** of people who wanted to win an argument.

The third lesson is that the **result** is never the same as the **question**. I have spent much of my life reading **reports** about things that are not the thing I actually wanted to know. This is not a failure of the reports. It is a failure of the questions, and the questions are mine.

A **study** says something about a **case** in particular. It does not say what that case means for me. That last step — from the study to the **idea** in your head — is the part where the mistakes are. An **example** is the same trap. One example is a picture of a thing, and a picture **shows** you the shape and hides the size. **Such** a small step, and nobody teaches it.

I have learned to ask one question of everything: what would have to be true for this to be false? If the answer is easy, the thing is weak. If the answer is impossible, be careful. It is **important** to **follow** the small footnotes. Yes, this is the whole method. It is dull and it works.

## The People in the Room

I have a **family** that I love and a **friend** who has known me since I was a **child**, and a **woman** I was nearly married to, and a mother who is eighty-one and still the most frightening person in any room.

The family is the **book** of my life. I do not mean the good parts only. I mean the arguments, the two Christmases nobody came, the wedding that did not happen, the letter I did not write for six years and then wrote in one hour and sent before I could think about it. I do not have a good **relationship** with any of it, and I do not need one. It is the raw material.

The friend is a different thing. He is the only person I can say anything to without first **looking** at the room. When I am in **trouble** he does not offer a solution; he asks what I have already tried, and then he **waits** until I have answered, and about a third of the time the answer arrives in the middle of his waiting. I have looked for the trick four years and there is no trick. Every good thing I have done got there because somebody **helped** me, and I have **left** that out of almost every story I have told about myself.

The woman I was nearly married to, her name was Sara. I still have her letters. When I read them I am struck by how **young** the person writing was, and how **certain**, and how completely right about the things that were not about us. That is what I think **history** is: people who were right about the small things and wrong about the one large thing.

My mother is a **woman** who has never once said "I am proud of you," but she has said, "that soup was better than last time," nine times in twenty years, and I understand that perfectly. She was a school **teacher** for thirty years and never once **telled** me what I was good at. When I finally **asked** her, last **week**, she said, "you are good at **staying** with a thing." I have carried that sentence for seven days and I have not put it down. She has had **breast** cancer twice and **carbon** in her lungs from forty years of other people's smoke, and she has never once used either of those to win an argument. That is **coach** behaviour, though she would hate the word.

And I have my own **person** to keep, which took me a long time to learn. I am a man who works too much and **asks** too little, and I have been told this by three different women in three different **cities**. It is a **relief** to be told, actually. It means somebody is still **there**.

Being a person in a **group** is the hardest work there is. You have to stay **small** enough for the room and **big** enough for yourself, and the distance between those two numbers changes by the hour. And you have to do it while **both** things are true at once.

## The Long Middle

Nobody writes about the middle of a life. The beginning gets a story and the end gets a **lesson**, and the middle gets a **week** in which you **pay** a bill, **call** a mother, **meet** a friend, and **try** to remember what it was all for. I did not understand this until I was about thirty-five. Before that I was waiting for the life to **start**, and then I understood that the waiting **was** the life.

The middle is not made of decisions. Big decisions are rare, and they arrive when everything else is already decided for you. What fills it is small **choice** after small choice — this **job** or that one, this **city** or the one after, this **word** or the one that is more honest.

Most of those choices are wrong. I have to **admit** that first, because the fact is the only ground I have. But a wrong choice you understand is worth more than a right choice you did not **look** at. Understanding is a kind of **point**, and a point can be a **turn**.

The **problem** of a middle life is that you cannot see it from the inside. You need somebody outside to tell you where you are. So I ask. I asked my friend and I asked Sara and I asked my mother, and none of them told me the truth, and all of them told me something true.

If I had to say it in one line, it would be this: most people are not trying to be happy and not trying to be successful, they are trying to be **clear**. Clear about what they are doing with the **week**, with the **year**, with the money and the **health** that will not come back. The rest of it — the **house**, the **look** of a **car**, whether people **like** you at the office — is decoration. Good decoration. Worth money. But decoration.

A man I **meet** once a month in a **bar** near the station has been doing this for **thirty** years and he is the only person I know who has managed it. He does not **want** more. He **spends** his **money** on a **small** **house** with a bad roof and a **good** view, and he can tell you off the top of his head what he will do next Tuesday. That is the whole trick, and there is no trick. When I was younger I thought a life like that was a failure of ambition. I do not think so any more. **Government**, money, age — the world puts a **rate** on all of us, and the ones who **seem** most restless are often running from a number they got a long time ago.

## Everything Holds Together

This is what I mean when I say everything holds together.

Not the job, which is **right** there and may be wrong. Not the city, which is one of six and no more real than the other five. Not the name, which is a set of sounds that other people use to get your attention.

What holds together is the sentence. A **reason** you can say out loud. A **form** of words that fits the **case** in front of you. A small rule you keep every day, like the rule about the screen before eight, or the rule that you are not allowed to **put** a decision off because today is a bad day for it.

A sentence is the smallest **system** that can **include** you. You do not have to hire anyone, you do not have to be in a **group**, you do not have to have any **money** at all. You just have to be able to say the **true** thing, and to **feel** that it is true, and to say it again the next day, when the feeling is smaller but the thing has not changed.

That is a very low bar and almost nobody clears it, because it requires you to give up the small pleasure of being **wrong** and the small pleasure of not knowing.

Here is the last thing I want to say, and I say it without hope, because I no longer have that and I do not miss it. What holds together is not the truth. It is the sentence you use while you are still deciding whether it is true. You will probably find out later that you were wrong, and you will **move** on and **change** it, and you will keep doing that, **again** and again, until one evening you look back and see that you did not change so much as you only became **able** to see. I went **into** this life with a small number of sentences and I came out of the first half with a better small number. That is all the difference I can report.

That is all. A **point** of light in a **dark** room, a sentence, a **reason** to keep the light on. It is not **far** from a kind of peace, though I never went for it and I am not **proud** of what I have. I only mean that a man with a **hand** on the switch and a sentence in his mouth is safer than a man with a **government** behind him and no sentence at all. I have been the second kind. It is a **sad** and **lonely** way to be, and it does not have to be.

So I will **say** the thing and let it stand. If you are at the beginning of this, or in the **long** middle of it, my whole advice is five words and you have heard them already: **write it down and read it back**. A **system** this simple will not **increase** your **service** to anybody, and it will not look **like** much from the **outside**, and I **promise** you it is enough. **Yes** — that is the **problem**, and it is the **issue**, and it is the one **fact** I have about people: almost nobody **provides** the world with anything at all, and the ones who do are not usually the ones we expected. It might be you on a **new** Tuesday, in a **new** **room**, with one clear sentence. I have to **believe** that a little, or I would not keep going.

If you want to know how the smaller parts of this actually work, they are in the pieces I wrote before: [A Day in the Life](01-%E6%97%A5%E5%B8%B8%E7%94%9F%E6%B4%BB%20A%20Day%20in%20the%20Life.md), [How Work Actually Works](02-%E5%B7%A5%E4%BD%9C%20How%20Work%20Actually%20Works.md), [The World Outside the Window](09-%E8%87%AA%E7%84%B6%20The%20World%20Outside%20the%20Window.md). This is only the thread that runs through them. The thread is thinner than the cloth. But it is the reason the cloth holds.
