# The Shape of a City

城市到底长什么样：一条街道、一班公交、一堆垃圾、几栋公共建筑，和住在里面的人。

## The First Hour

I once lived in a small **city** in the north, in a country that does not enjoy winter. My room was on the fourth floor, and the local **bus** woke me before the alarm did. Generally the streets were empty at six. Once, in fact, they were so quiet that I could hear the water in the pipes.

The **population** was small, perhaps a hundred thousand **residents**, but it grew every year. People came in from the **villages** around it, and they brought new **material** with them: new shops, new ideas, new noise. The old **town** centre had stood for three hundred years. Everything past it was **modern**, and a good deal less **beautiful**.

A city teaches you its **directions** early — **north**, **south**, **east**, **west**. My flat faced north, so no sun came through the window. But on **recent** **summer** evenings I would carry a chair down to the yard and sit there anyway, just to feel the light. There is something **real** about the first hour of a day, and a city will give it to you cheaply if you are willing to get up.

The **community** in my **district** was mostly **male**: men going to work, and grandmothers who did not. **Everybody** knew the man who sold bread. I could never remember his name, and I **wish** I had asked him. His shop stood at the corner, and he **bear** the winter like everyone else — hands cracked, voice low, same grey coat. He did not say **nice** things often, and I did not know how to say them either. That is **real** life, not a story.

## Streets Are the Real Map

A **road** is not a road. It is a river, and the buildings on both sides are its banks. In my city the main **street** ran east from the **station** to the **square**, and almost everything worth seeing stood along it. Behind the church a **path** climbed the hill, and it was narrower and better. In the northern **block** you could hear somebody playing **tennis** on a Sunday, which told you more about the place than any **gallery** could.

Walk **ahead** for an hour and the **districts** sort themselves into an order. Near the station: **hotels**, a **restaurant** with photographs of the food on the wall, a **library** that stays open late. Two streets further on: a **market**, a school, a small **theater** with a red sign. The **square** belongs to nobody, so everybody uses it — students, old men, kids on bikes, a man who sells nothing at all.

The **old** **castle** sat above the river with a **gate** that was locked most of the year. A **portrait** of some nineteenth-century officer hung in the guard house, and his **nose** had been scratched by somebody's dog in 1954. That is what makes the place real: not the building, but the small damage.

Then there is the **construction**. For two years a **site** at the end of our street was a hole, then a row of **columns**, then a **tower** of twelve floors. Every morning a **truck** arrived and the whole **block** listened to it. The new **bridge** over the river took longer still, and when it opened, people walked out onto it at night just to see the lights. A city is never finished. It is always in the middle of a sentence, and you happen to be one of the words.

## Getting Around

The **traffic** began at seven and never really stopped. There was one bus line down the middle of the town, one **train** in each direction every twenty minutes, and a fast **tube** service to the **capital** three hours away. A **taxi** was cheap, but you had to **wait** for one at the stand. A **bike** was cheaper. A **van** carried what the truck could not.

A **passenger** who needed to cross town in a hurry learned to love the bus: about twenty cents **per** ride, and forty minutes of free reading. She would **drive** her van at six in the morning and be at the far side of the city before the shops opened. The bus company kept a **stock** of twelve old **b**uses, four of which worked and one of which smelled of fish.

The **noise** was the hard part. In summer the **tires** went **heavy** with water and the windows fogged, and the only **gas** station at the north end was open at four in the morning, which is either a comfort or a warning. The last bus continued **past** the hospital and out into the fields, where the **port** lights and the **harbor** cranes stood in the dark. Out there the city looked like a thing you had only seen in a film, and the cold woke you up.

Weather changes the machine too. After a week of dry wind the **dust** lay on every **windshield** in town, and the city turned the same pale **gray** colour. You learn to **pick** your routes by shade, **raise** a window when the wind changes, and accept that **whatever** you plan, the weather has already decided it.

## The Buildings That Serve Us

Every **center** has the same short list of **buildings**, and a city is honest about them. There is the **town** **hall**, where the **council** meets on Thursday and anyone may sit at the back. There is a police **station** and a fire **station**, and a small **hospital** with an **emergency** door facing the main road, because that is where the accidents happen. There is the **museum**, free on the first Sunday, and the art **gallery** above it, which is not free at all.

There is a **library** with a reading room that smells of paper and dust. Nine other people sat in it most afternoons, all doing exactly the same thing, and the **attendance** was never discussed. There is a **hotel** that business men go to for lunch. There is a **park** with a **gate** at each corner, a **theater** with red seats, and a square of grey stone that turns gold for forty minutes at the end of the day.

And there is the **bin**. Every city is a machine that **produces** **waste**, and the bin is where the machine confesses. A good **local** council takes them away twice a day. A bad one sends its **collection** round down one street out of three, and you learn to time your **walk** accordingly. Whoever picks up the bags is **someone** whose name nobody ever records.

Waste is a good place to look at how a city actually **operates**. The **mechanism** is simple enough: somebody **produces** it, somebody **collects** it, somebody pays. What is hard is the **addition** of one more **collection** round, the **lack** of a plan, the endless **stuff** on the pavement that nobody will claim as their own.

I once asked the **agent** at the ticket office which **route** was quickest. He said he would **recommend** the one he took himself. That **suggestion** was better than any map, because it came from a **scene** he knew by heart.

## Things Go Wrong

Every few years something breaks. A pipe goes under a main road and the whole **center** has no water for two days. A **disaster** of the boring kind — a wall, a sinkhole, a gas leak — turns an ordinary morning into a **news** paper front page.

In one summer a **storm** took the roof off the sports **hall**, and the **collapse** was watched on television by people who knew the man who built it, thirty years ago, and were not **surprised**. There is always an **investigation** afterwards. It is usually a man with a **notebook** and a **copy** of a list.

The **anger** is real but it passes; what stays is **disorder** — a door that never gets fixed, a light that never gets replaced, a road that becomes a place where you no longer want to **pose** for a photograph. And **pollution** is the slowest disaster of all: it does not break anything, so nobody investigates it, and yet it **destroys** more health than a fire.

Longer than anger is **depression**. In an **industrial** town when the factory closes, there is no **existence** for the young people to walk into. **Violence** rises a little. **Destruction** rises a little more. The population leaves, the **rent** falls, the shops close, and the city gets quieter in a way that is not peaceful.

## Who Runs the Place

The **mayor** is chosen every four years and most people cannot name him. The **council** has thirteen members and meets in a room with bad heating. A **citizen** who wants something changed has to write a letter, and a letter must arrive as an **envelope** addressed to somebody, because a general complaint is a **disaster** for the office.

They write back, too. They always write back. The reply contains the word **ad** vertisement three times, a number, and a **suggestion** that you enquire about the matter. If you **cite** the council's own policy, something may actually move. This is not cynicism; it is a working knowledge of how a gate operates.

What the city owes its people is simple to say and hard to price: a safe route from home to work, clean air, a school within walking distance, a public square where nobody has to buy anything. What it charges for all of this is **rent**, and rent rises in an **inflation** that feels like weather. A landlord in a **rural** village pays a fifth of what a resident in the **urban** part of the same country pays for the same square metres. **Rural** and urban are not two places; they are two prices.

Every crisis begins with a **liability** nobody wants to **possession** , and nobody wants to be **possessed** by it. A **disaster** happens, the newspaper finds a **witness**, the witness finds a **blame** -worthy name, and the **coverage** lasts nine days. Then comes the **inquiry**, and then the **reduction** in funding. What the **council** **promises** is almost never what it delivers; that gap is the **stability** on which local government actually rests.

## The People in Between

A city is not a machine; it is a living thing, and the parts that matter most are almost never on the map. There is a **farmer** who brings vegetables on Thursdays and complains about the price as if the two of them were old friends. There is a **musician** who plays outside the station and keeps a **collection** of hats from other countries. There is a **producer** of nothing at all but a very good product: gossip.

There is a **physical** limit to any city. There is a **practical** limit too — the hour when the last bus goes, the size of a flat, the **reasonable** distance a person can walk in August. These limits **shape** behaviour in ways that no **philosophy** can argue away, and most arguments about city life are really arguments about the weather and the walls.

A **twin** I knew left for the capital and became a **master** of something complicated. He sent money home, then stopped. There was a long **mess** of feelings, a long silence, then an **invitation**, and then — I still do not know how — we were friends again. Family is the most **dependent** structure a person can be in, and the most resilient.

In the evenings the **entertainment** is cheap and public. A **concert** in the square. A football match that fills the **stadium** or, more likely, the corner of it where the men sit. A **boy** with a **ball** and a concrete wall. A **pig** in a **shed** behind the industrial estate, which is a **controversial** topic: a **genuine** smell and a real noise.

## What a City Is For

I do not have a full answer, but I have an **opinion**, and it is plain enough. The **landscape** of a city is its skyline seen from the west on a grey morning, its **shade** at noon, and the way its lights **shine** through the **darkness** at six. All of that is physical, and it matters, but it is not the point.

The point is **dimension**: a city is a place where you can **select** a life you could not have selected anywhere else. You can hear a concert you cannot afford, read in a library for free, eat a meal for the price of a bus ticket, and **consult** a person whose whole life has been your **existence** in the world. You can be a **fool** on Saturday and a genuine scholar on Monday, and nobody will ask you to choose in advance.

A city is also the fastest way to become dependent on people you did not choose. It can be **mess** y and **cheek** y, and it can make you **hunger** y as well as full. But mostly it simply **attracts** people, the way a light does, and the reason is a bit boring and quite important at the same time: because everything is **nearby** and nothing is certain.

So here is the **suggestion** I would make, in the end, and it is not a **philosophy** at all, only a **habit**: **trace** one **street** carefully, on foot, once a week, for a year. Look at the doors, the windows, the **shade**, the **smell** from the restaurant at number forty. You will find that a **city** is not a set of **buildings** but a set of **habits**, and that the two are much harder to change from each other than most people believe.

You will also find, **hopefully**, that somebody is doing the same walk from the other **end**. In my **opinion** that is the whole story of a city, **dear** reader, and it is **presumably** why they keep building **more** of them.

## A Small Coda on Small Cities

**Someone** once asked me whether I would go back. I could not answer **surely**, because the answer kept changing with the weather, and *sure* is not a **reference** to anything.

What I can say is this. A small city is a place where every **variation** in your week is **visible**. A friend is ill, you know within the hour. A new shop opens, **everybody** has an opinion by evening. The **crowd** on the main street is the same crowd you saw yesterday, and the **prospect** of leaving is both terrible and oddly **rewarding**, because you are sure the door stays open.

That closeness has a cost. It is **difficult** to keep a secret, harder to **bury** anything. Every **episode** becomes a **scene** with a **cast**, and the **latter** half of the story belongs to somebody else entirely. **Whilst** nobody means harm, the **damage** is often done — **characterize** a small town this way and you will not be far wrong.

A **celebration** of a **victory** in the square can fill a street that feels empty all year. A good **album**, a good **concert**, a good **editor** who will **edit** your work for free — these **highlight** the **educational** **prospect** of a small town far better than any **expansion** plan. A **bloody** awful winter, by contrast, is enough to put the **penalty** of staying somewhere on the whole town at once.

So here is my **commitment**, such as it is. I will **enable** myself to **continue** walking. I will **proceed** slowly. I will search for the ordinary, and I will try to **demonstrate** that the ordinary was there all along.

And if I fail, if I am **embarrassed**, if I am **overcome** by a **hunger** for somewhere else — **cheek** by **cheek**, shoulder to shoulder — well. A city that cannot **comprise** both is only a **drawing**. And a drawing, however **beautiful**, has no weather, and that is why it is not enough.

A city has to **survive** you. That is its whole **challenge**, and it does it so well you never notice.

One more thing, **plus** it is the part I think about most. The best **instance** I know of a city doing this well is a street market that opens once a week on the edge of town. Traders **distribute** their goods; regulars **link** with them across years; the council **recruits** nobody but **tackles** the traffic. Nobody **implys** it will last. It is, **typically** , far too loud, and it **alters** the whole **entry** to the neighborhood.

It also **ensures** that a **considerable** number of people meet each other who otherwise never would. That is not a small thing. **Luck** has a name, and in a small city it has a **face** — somebody you already **know**, standing in the rain, deciding to wait for you. If you want to **contact** them, most traders give out a card. There are **dozens** of them along the row by then, and the price of anything has **decreased** at least once, whatever the **opponent** -shop at the far end would like you to believe.

**Unlike** a **plenty** -fed imagination, the real mechanism is thin, fragile and mostly dull, and your **brain** will supply the rest **fairly** willingly. Get there early, and **secure** a good spot at the **entry** — the market shuts at four, **increasingly** , they say, and I am **successfully** out of time.

If you want to **fulfill** that habit, do it **throughout** the year.
